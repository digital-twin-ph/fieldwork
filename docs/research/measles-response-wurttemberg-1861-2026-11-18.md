# Hagelloch watched measles spread and closed nothing

_Created 2026-11-18 · Updated 2026-11-18_

Asked by the practitioner on 2026-11-18, for experiment [220](../experiments/220-a-disease-model-configured-from-its-evidence.md)'s
7-1-7 spike: *"What would be the response in the era?"* Written by a research pass of three parts and a report writer,
then checked against the sources the practitioner approved. The seven authored notes in the Hagelloch project draw on
it. Closed sources are named and cited only; no copy of any is in the repository.

In Hagelloch in 1861–62, the response to measles was a study, not a control effort. **The first child fell ill on 30 October 1861.** News of the "red spots" reached the Tübingen hospital in November, on a day no source gives. **The first field visit was on 29 November (day 30)**, when the Tübingen district physician (Oberamtsarzt) brought the student Pfeilsticker to the village. Daily visits followed from 30 November to 18 December. The open text of Pfeilsticker's 1863 dissertation records **no school closure, no formal report to an authority, no disinfection and no limit on church or gatherings**. The school stayed open. The schoolmaster sent home children who were visibly ill, and children with a rash stayed away. One farmer shut his own children away for the whole epidemic. We found **no evidence that measles was notifiable in Württemberg in 1861**. The one Württemberg reporting list found (1883) leaves measles out. For a 7-1-7 score, only the response date is cited. Detection is known only to the month. Notification is not recorded at all. Most of the regulatory picture rests on search snippets and inference, because the key law books sat behind bot checks.

All evidence below is open unless marked. "Snippet only" means a search-engine summary of a page we did not open. Treat those as leads, not facts. Day numbers count from 30 October 1861 as day 0, as the R `surveillance` package does.

## Measles was probably not notifiable, and no 1861 rule names it

The Württemberg health system ran through districts. **Each Oberamt (district) had an Oberamtsarzt (district physician) from 1814** ([Wikipedia, Oberamt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberamt_(W%C3%BCrttemberg))). His founding instruction of 1814 required **one general report a year, due each January** (§ 9). Decrees of 1819 and 1846 set the form of these yearly reports ([Archivalia](https://archivalia.hypotheses.org/172790)). That yearly report is the only reporting duty with a stated deadline that we found for 1861.

The reporting chain is documented only later. An 1883 ministry text says that **"larger epidemics" of typhus, typhoid, dysentery, diphtheria and scarlet fever must be reported by the village head (Ortsvorstand, the Schultheiß) to the Oberamt**. The Oberamt and the Oberamtsarzt then report to the Ministry of the Interior. **Measles is not on the list** ([Amtsblatt 1883, Staatsbibliothek Berlin](https://digital-beta.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de/werkansicht?PPN=PPN74658587X&PHYSID=PHYS_0115&view=fulltext-endless), snippet only). Note that this is a list of epidemics to report, not of single cases. Even in 1914, measles cases were reportable in only "some" German states ([Dochow 1914, Wikisource](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesundheitspolizei); [Deutschland unter Kaiser Wilhelm II, 1914](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Seite:Deutschland_unter_Kaiser_Wilhelm_II_Band_3.pdf/285)).

So the likely answer is: **measles was not notifiable as a single case in 1861**. This is derived, not cited. If an outbreak was reported at all, the path would run from the Schultheiß to the Oberamt Tübingen, with the Oberamtsarzt alongside, and on to the medical board. The board's files on "epidemic and contagious diseases" are in Staatsarchiv Ludwigsburg, E 162 I ([Archivalia](https://archivalia.hypotheses.org/172790)). No one has checked them for Hagelloch. We found no reporting duty on private doctors or on the pastor.

Prussia offers a contrast. Its Regulativ of 8 August 1835 covered measles. It required a report to the police **only for "malignant or numerous" cases** of measles, scarlet fever and rubella. It also allowed isolation, school closure and bans on gatherings ([Klein, FU Berlin diss.](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/3478/Diss_S.Klein.pdf), snippet only; [Umehara diss., Düsseldorf](https://docserv.uni-duesseldorf.de/servlets/DerivateServlet/Derivate-19286/Umehara_Diss_zur%20PDF_Publikation_26April2011.pdf)). Sources disagree on the section number (§ 59 or § 14) and on the day (8 or 18 August). We found no Württemberg rule like it before 1870.

## The school stayed open; the schoolmaster sent sick children home

**No source we could read records a closure of the Hagelloch school.** Pfeilsticker's text shows school in session through the epidemic. "Of 50 sick, 42 go to school." Children came home "from school or from church" with the first symptoms. One "went to school with it". One coughed in church ([Pfeilsticker 1863, OCR text](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)).

Exclusion was informal. **"Many could hardly be kept from school and had to be sent home again by the schoolmaster."** Parents and teachers assured Pfeilsticker that **no child with a rash attended** during the first wave ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)). One child was back at school on day 3 of recovery (one case, OCR-dependent). We found no exclusion of healthy brothers and sisters. The decision maker was the schoolmaster himself. No order from any authority is recorded.

One modern claim cuts the other way. A summary of "A Network-based Analysis of the 1861 Hagelloch Measles Data" says **the school closed late, when 27% of pupils already had symptoms**, with no real effect ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221860757_A_Network-based_Analysis_of_the_1861_Hagelloch_Measles_Data), page returned 403; snippet only). We could not see its date or its source. Other modern papers treat closure only as a "what if" simulation. Until this claim is traced to Pfeilsticker, to Oesterle's 1992 dissertation, or to a village record, **a model should not declare a historical closure**.

Who could have closed the school? We found no rule. The 1836 Württemberg school law put village schools under church oversight ([Stadtarchiv Ulm overview](https://stadtarchiv.ulm.de:443/-/media/archiv/downloads/ugin/schulen/schulen-19-20-jahrhundert-ueberblick.pdf), snippet only). That makes **the pastor, as local school inspector, or the Oberamt on the Oberamtsarzt's advice**, the likely authorities. This is inferred from structure only. In Prussia, the Landrat ordered closures, but that is 1880s and later practice ([Meyers Lexikon via Wörterbuchnetz](http://woerterbuchnetz.de/Meyers/call_wbgui_py_from_form?sigle=Meyers&mode=Volltextsuche&hitlist=&patternlist=&lemid=IS06226), snippet only). A neighbouring Baden village closed its school for measles and other diseases in 1875–76 ([Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek](https://www.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/item/YP3IZ6MC7F4CK6OR3AY5AJN7REBUALUW), snippet only). That is not Württemberg, and it is 14 years later.

## Two rooms, two age groups, one schoolmaster

The school is well described in outline. **"The schoolhouse, built 1827, contains two schoolrooms; the schoolmaster has a separate dwelling"** ([Oberamtsbeschreibung Tübingen 1867, Wikisource](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Beschreibung_des_Oberamts_Tübingen/Kapitel_B_12)). Pfeilsticker writes that **"the whole school is divided into two sections"**. One held children of about 6 to 9, plus some 10-year-olds. The other held ages 10 to 14. Each had its own room ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)). These match the dataset's "1st class" and "2nd class". Preschool children are the third group.

**The younger section was infected all at once in the first wave.** The first peak, around 21 November (day 22), "belonged entirely to the school". The older section and the preschool children followed ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt); [EpiCompare vignette](https://skgallagher.github.io/EpiCompare/articles/not-built-vignettes/hagelloch-eda.html)). One 7-year-old first-class boy is suspected of infecting about 30 others. A recent paper gives class sizes of **90, 30 and 65** among 185 susceptible children, but does not label which is which ([arXiv 2603.21992](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21992)). Matching them to classes is our inference.

Staffing is less clear. The schoolmaster (Schulmeister) is named. Pfeilsticker writes "teachers" in the plural. A "young trainee teacher" (Lehrerszögling, or Lehrersgehülfe in another passage) served as guide ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)). One master plus one assistant, one per room, fits the facts. No source states it.

**The school calendar is unknown.** We found no open source for school days per week, daily hours, autumn harvest holidays or Christmas holidays in 1861. Whether the first-class wave matched the start of a winter school term is a real question for the model. The notes raise a Martinmas (11 November) start as a lead but could not source it.

## Care came from Tübingen, a good hour's walk away

**No physician lived in Hagelloch.** The 1867 district book lists none, though it names one for nearby Gönningen ([Kap. B 12](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Beschreibung_des_Oberamts_Tübingen/Kapitel_B_12); [Kap. B 10](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Beschreibung_des_Oberamts_Tübingen/Kapitel_B_10)). The village had **577 people present in 1861**. It lay "a good hour" from Tübingen. Medical help "is mostly sought in the Tübingen hospital" ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)).

That explains detection. News "came to the hospital in November 1861, already after the first illnesses with the red spots". Then "the cases soon multiplied". **Professor Niemeyer decided "to take the whole epidemic in hand from the hospital"** ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)). The text gives no day and does not say who carried the news.

The field response then ran as follows. **On 29 November, the Oberamtsarzt, "in his official capacity", took Pfeilsticker to Hagelloch.** He introduced him to the pastor, the Schultheiß and the schoolmaster. All three received him "with the greatest friendliness". **From 30 November to 18 December, Pfeilsticker visited every morning.** Niemeyer came every Sunday and Thursday. The trainee teacher **took a census twice**, house by house, by age and sex. He also found each child's rash date on his own, and Pfeilsticker checked these against his notes. Most cases were seen in person. A few early cases, already recovered, were taken from history alone ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)).

Families wanted treatment, not study. Pfeilsticker writes that many houses would have shut their doors if the team had only taken temperatures. Treatment was comfort care: sweetened teas, dilute acid, sometimes quinine for high fever. He judged it "quite indifferent" to the outcome. Some children died before the team arrived, for example one on 18 November ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)). The case fatality was **6.4% (12 of 187)**, and 10% under age 5 ([Aaby, Thoma & Dietz 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:35182549&resultType=core&format=json); closed, abstract only).

Other measures were absent or private. **Farmer W. Seyboldt shut his children away for the whole epidemic**, and three escaped infection. Inside houses, isolation was "hardly conceivable", since siblings shared one bed or one room. Church went on with infectious children present. **Disinfection, fumigation, house placards and burial rules are not mentioned** ([Pfeilsticker 1863](https://archive.org/stream/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog/beitrgezurpatho00pfeigoog_djvu.txt)). Pfeilsticker saw school spread as near certain: it happens "so easily and on so grand a scale".

## Scoring Hagelloch against 7-1-7

The 7-1-7 targets are 7 days from emergence to detection, 1 day from detection to notification of a public health authority, and 7 days from notification to early response. Here is how Hagelloch fits.

**Emergence** is cited: 30 October 1861, day 0. **Detection** is cited only to the month. If news reached Tübingen on 1 November, detection took 2 days. If it came on 29 November, it took 30. The words "already after the first illnesses" lean early. Pfeilsticker also writes that cases "soon multiplied" after the news, which suggests the news came before the 21 November peak. So detection likely falls between days 2 and 22. That range is derived. The 7-day target is met only if news arrived by 6 November. We cannot say if it did.

**Notification** has no record. The Oberamtsarzt is the nearest thing to a public health authority. He knew by 29 November at the latest, since he led the visit. Whether he heard from the village, the hospital or Niemeyer is unknown. No formal notice was required, as far as we can tell. The 1-day target cannot be scored. It is best declared "not applicable: no duty to notify" or as an assumed lag.

**Early response** is cited: 29 November for the field visit, 30 November for daily surveillance. That is day 30. Measured from the latest possible detection, response took 0 days. From an early-November detection, it took up to 28 days. Note what the response was. It was case finding, a census and comfort care. **No control action was taken.** Under 7-1-7, an investigation counts as an early response action. A model should still be clear that nothing was done to stop spread.

The honest summary is this. Hagelloch fails "7" for response from emergence (30 days), whatever the split. The split between detection and response is unknown, because the November day is missing.

## Conclusion

Hagelloch is a clean case of an unchecked epidemic, and that is what makes it useful. The record supports a model with almost no response layer: no notification duty, no closure, no disinfection, and a school that stayed open while the schoolmaster sent the sickest home. The single observed "response" was a university study. It arrived after the school wave had peaked. Its value was the data it left, not the cases it prevented.

For 7-1-7, the key missing fact is one date: the November day the news reached Tübingen. That day alone splits the 30-day gap between detection and response. Reading the Pfeilsticker page images is the cheapest way to look for it. The second open question, the "late closure" claim, matters more. If it holds up, the model needs a closure event at a cited date. If it does not, the model should state plainly that the school never closed.

### Pfeilsticker 1863, printed page 7 (PDF page 10), checked against the image

> "Wie sich bei der Armuth des Ortes leicht erklären lässt, wird meist die ärztliche Hilfe im Tübinger Krankenhaus
> gesucht und so kam es auch, dass im November 1861 schon nach den ersten Erkrankungen an den „rothen Flecken"
> Nachricht davon in's Krankenhaus kam; bald häuften sich die Fälle so, dass der Vorstand der medicinischen Klinik
> … Professor Niemeyer, sich entschloss, die ganze Epidemie vom Krankenhaus aus in die Hand zu nehmen …"

"As the village's poverty easily explains, medical help is mostly sought at the Tübingen hospital, and so it happened
that in November 1861, already after the first illnesses with the 'red spots', news of it reached the hospital; soon
the cases multiplied so that the head of the medical clinic, Professor Niemeyer, decided to take the whole epidemic in
hand from the hospital."

> "Noch an dem gleichen Tage (es war am 29. November) fuhren wir in Begleitung des Herrn Oberamtsarztes von Tübingen
> nach Hagelloch hinaus."

"That same day (it was 29 November) we drove out to Hagelloch with the district physician of Tübingen."

**What this settles:**

- **No day of detection is given.** The OCR reading was right; the page image confirms it. Detection stays "November
  1861", days 2 to 30.
- **How detection happened:** through families seeking care at the Tübingen hospital, the village's usual source of
  medical help, "explained by its poverty". So the detection pathway in 1861 is **care-seeking**, cited; the teacher
  pathway is not what detected it.
- **29 November is the day of the decision and the first visit**, "that same day" as Niemeyer's proposal. The news
  came earlier, the cases "soon multiplied", then the decision.
- The OCR quotes used in the authored notes for this passage are correct in substance.

### Corrections to the report above

- **Detection:** the report derives days 2 to 22 from Pfeilsticker's wording. That reading is weak. Experiment 220 and the
  Hagelloch notes use days 2 to 30, the whole of November, until a source gives the day.
- **The detection pathway is cited:** families seeking care at the Tübingen hospital (Pfeilsticker, printed page 7, checked
  against the page image), not a teacher or an official.
- **The "closed at 27%" claim** traced to no source at all; see Groendyke above.
