Remote Sensing Epidemiology: FieldWork Research Note

Date: 2026-11-05 (UTC; date-level timestamp)

Purpose

Identify open-data epidemiological worked examples requiring satellite remote sensing, and specify reproducible FieldWork workflows linking observations, exposures, analysis, provenance, and manuscript outputs. Data availability must be checked at file/schema/license level before implementation.

Recommended primary example: Brazil dengue

Research question: Do satellite-derived temperature and vegetation measures improve prediction of municipal dengue incidence beyond seasonal patterns and historical notifications?

Candidate datasets:

● InfoDengue surveillance: https://info.dengue.mat.br/

● Mosqlimate data: https://sprint.mosqlimate.org/data/

● Candidate integrated dataset: https://zenodo.org/records/22029053 (verify record, files, license)

● Candidate code: https://github.com/jssanchez-garreta/dataset-multimodal-dengue-brazil (verify repository)

● MODIS products: https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/

● CHIRPS: https://www.chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps

● IBGE boundaries: https://www.ibge.gov.br/en/geosciences/territorial-organization/territorial-meshes.html

Data schema: municipality_id, epi_year, epi_week, cases, population, lst_day_c, lst_night_c, ndvi, rainfall_mm, source_version, observation_date, availability_date, qa_flag.

Computations: satellite QA masking; zonal statistics; population-weighted exposure; epidemiological week alignment; incidence per 100,000; 0–12-week exposure lags; negative-binomial regression with population offset; distributed-lag nonlinear models; spatial autocorrelation; blocked temporal validation; predictive comparison with seasonality-only baselines.

Safeguards: LST is not air temperature; avoid future-information leakage; distinguish modeled exposure from observed health outcomes; account for reporting changes, ecological fallacy, spatial confounding, and uncertainty.

Deliverables: exposure maps, epidemic curves, lag-response plots, out-of-time validation, data dictionary, provenance manifest, manuscript methods/results sections.

Kenya malaria

Question: Are rainfall and vegetation anomalies associated with subsequent malaria burden by subcounty?

Sources: CHIRPS, ERA5-Land, MODIS, administrative boundaries, population denominators, and an authorized/verified subcounty health series.

Methods: monthly climate anomalies, lagged exposures, incidence when counts and denominators exist, hierarchical spatiotemporal regression, spatial residual checks.

Gate: Do not substitute modeled malaria prevalence for observed monthly cases without labeling it as modeled.

Bangladesh cholera and flooding

Question: Does flood exposure precede increased cholera incidence?

Sources: Sentinel-1 SAR, JRC Global Surface Water, CHIRPS/GPM rainfall, population, water/sanitation covariates, and verified geocoded cholera surveillance.

Methods: flood area proportion, exposure buffers, temporal lags, regression or event studies with explicit assumptions.

Gate: Confirm an open and legally reusable cholera outcome series; otherwise implement only flood-exposure mapping.

European heat and mortality

Question: How do extreme heat and urban greenness relate to short-term mortality?

Sources: ERA5/ERA5-Land, MODIS LST, land cover, demographic data, public mortality records where available.

Methods: heatwave definitions, percentile anomalies, quasi-Poisson time series, distributed-lag nonlinear models, attributable fractions and uncertainty.

Gate: Verify mortality spatial/temporal coverage; do not treat LST as direct human ambient-temperature exposure.

US satellite-informed air pollution and respiratory outcomes

Question: Are satellite-informed PM2.5 exposures associated with variation in aggregate respiratory outcomes?

Sources: CDC Environmental Public Health Tracking, EPA AirData, NASA Earthdata, Census boundaries/population.

Methods: exposure aggregation, age standardization where supported, ecological regression, spatial diagnostics, sensitivity to exposure measurement error and suppressed counts.

Gate: Check outcome suppression, reporting definitions, and whether PM2.5 estimates already assimilate ground monitoring data.

Nigeria malaria

Question: Do climate anomalies predict variation in malaria burden at LGA level?

Sources: GRID3 LGA boundaries, WorldPop, CHIRPS, ERA5-Land, MODIS vegetation, verified malaria data.

Methods: monthly climate cube, baseline anomalies, 0–3-month lags, negative-binomial or hierarchical models, spatial validation.

Gate: Observed monthly LGA case series are not assumed public. If only modeled burden exists, do not claim surveillance-based inference.

FieldWork architecture

Dataset catalogue / license validation

  -> Remote sensing acquisition (STAC, COG, raster tiles)

  -> QA masking and CRS/time alignment

  -> Zonal or population-weighted exposure aggregation

  -> Epidemiological space-time join

  -> Study-design-aware statistical modeling

  -> Spatial/temporal validation

  -> Figures, provenance, GitHub manuscript workflow

Suggested registry operations: discover_asset, validate_license, mask_quality, aggregate_exposure, align_epi_calendar, build_lags, fit_model, validate_temporally, export_provenance.

Store CRS, units, observation period, source retrieval date, version, uncertainty, and missingness for every transformation. Keep large imagery outside Git and version references/checksums. Never commit identifiable or restricted health data.

Implementation milestones

1. Data readiness: Verify Brazil source URLs, actual files, licenses, geographic identifiers, time coverage, and missingness.

2. Baseline: Reproduce dengue incidence and seasonality maps using prepared data.

3. Satellite pipeline: Independently derive at least one MODIS exposure from original observations; compare with supplied features.

4. Analysis: Fit lagged models, quantify uncertainty, and evaluate blocked temporal generalization against baselines.

5. Publication: Generate manuscript, figures, methods, and machine-readable provenance with GitHub review.

6. Generalization: Implement US PM2.5 example to test non-infectious environmental epidemiology.
