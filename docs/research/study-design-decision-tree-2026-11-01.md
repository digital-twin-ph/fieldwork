# Ask three questions before naming designs

_Created 2026-11-01 · Updated 2026-11-01_

**Standing of this note.** A proposal, and nothing in it is built. It replaces the first draft of this file, which carried a wrong Labrecque citation, a contested claim that density-sampled studies must report rate ratios, and natural experiment as a branch. Every source carries how it was read, and unread sources stay marked unread.

Ground the tree on three separate records, not one branching label: how subjects were sampled and observed (which yields one of Fieldwork's six declared values), who or what allocated exposure, and how time is structured. Open agency sources now back most of the design nodes and caveats, because CDC's Principles of Epidemiology course, WHO Basic Epidemiology and PAHO's MOPECE were read directly, and they separate designs by comparison group, who determines exposure, enrolment and timing. Their largest correction falls on the surveillance terminal: **no agency classification read lists surveillance as a study type**, so the note must stop saying "Declared as a surveillance study", and that sentence is one template string in `fieldwork/src/study-design.ts`. **Chapter 7 of the CDC Field Epidemiology Manual adds a third field design this tree could not route (below). German 2001 (MMWR 2001), Buehler 2004 and Manual chapters 1, 3, 6 and 8 have now been read directly, and they add no surveillance study type. None of the 15 named gated papers was read in full, and PMC's reCAPTCHA in the third round came before any of them was requested**, so the proposal claims nothing from them. The evidence remains strong for the case-control estimand algebra, moderate for cohort alignment, reporting guidance and surveillance artefacts, and thin for question order and the ecological branch. **No agency source read offers a decision tree ending in these six values, and ERIC and Europe PMC searches found no study testing a flowchart for design identification**, so the educational claim is a reasoned design and not a tested benefit. The update adds E6 on outcome recording, CC6 on control source and S7 on the comparison baseline, widens E1 for WHO's stated exceptions, and fixes a trap at D5 that would have sent CDC's interview-based retrospective cohort to `cross-sectional`. The Robinson figures and the two renderings of Mathes and Pieper are narrowed and stay flagged. "Not sure" and "not stated" must still stop the path without declaring, which Fieldwork cannot do while a new `study_design` node defaults to `design:'cohort'`. Nothing here has been built or tested with practitioners.

## Seven findings set the shape of the tree

**Published trees share a small question set, but none covers Fieldwork's six designs.** AHRQ's round 2 tree, DASHCI, the American Dietetic Association tool, RTI-UNC and SIGN all draw on comparison, assignment, randomisation, group count, timing and how groups were defined ([AHRQ report](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]; [SIGN](https://www.sign.ac.uk/assets/study_design.pdf) [Full]). "Ecological" appears nowhere in the AHRQ report, "surveillance" appears only for a rejected drug-surveillance tool, and the ADA tool routes "correlational" to noncomparative ([AHRQ report](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). Grimes and Schulz 2002 and Dekkers 2012 were not read, so what is said of them below is second-hand and marked so.

**Agency texts give axes and prose rules, not a tree.** CDC's course calls a comparison group "the key feature of analytic epidemiology", and separates experimental from observational by whether the investigator determines exposure, then observational designs by entry and timing ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct, whole section]). The closest thing to a rule is one sentence of outbreak advice: a retrospective cohort in a small, well-defined population with a full list, case-control when the population is not well defined and speed matters ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). PAHO gives a classification tree without questions ([MOPECE 5, Figure 5.1, p. 7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]), and WHO a suitability grid whose cell values could not be recovered from the extraction ([WHO Basic, Table 3.3, p. 49](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct, columns only]). This holds for everything read, including Manual chapter 7, which gives a prose choice rule and a benefits table (Table 7.1) and no tree. Chapters 1, 3, 6 and 8 add none either. **The agency texts also do not agree on terminals:** CDC names cohort, case-control and cross-sectional, with no ecological design and no use of the term case series ([SS1978 L1 §3](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section3.html) [Keyword]). WHO and PAHO name ecological and case series, and only PAHO files ecological as analytic.

**No head-to-head study shows which question order is best.** AHRQ users preferred opening with comparison because assignment copes poorly with mixed methods, and that is a stated preference, not a tested effect ([AHRQ report, p. 22](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]).

**Agreement is low on hard tasks and higher on narrow ones.** AHRQ measured Fleiss κ=0.26 with 13 categories on 30 purposively difficult studies, 0.33 among its reference raters, and 0.45 on a random 15 after revision, a rise that cannot be credited to the tool alone because the sample halved and half the testers changed ([AHRQ report, Table 4](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). A detailed-instruction tool reached κ=0.67 ([Seo 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.09.013) [Abstract]). A binary "true case series or not" call reached 100% between two raters on 64 papers ([Esene 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00381-014-2460-1) [Abstract]). That contrast supports short binary nodes, though no study tests six categories, and all of this measures reviewers reading papers, not practitioners describing their own study.

**The label audits show which boundary leaks most.** Self-labelled case-control papers were judged not case-control in 65% of 55 surgical papers ([Mihailovic 2005](https://www.canjsurg.ca/content/48/2/148) [Tool]), 67% of 339 orthopaedic papers ([LeBrun 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11420-020-09753-9) [Abstract]), 70.7% of 386 ophthalmology papers, of which 190 of 273 were cross-sectional, about 70% by arithmetic ([2026 audit](https://doi.org/10.1167/tvst.15.4.11) [Tool; its erratum not read]), and 97% of 86 rehabilitation papers against 34% of a 221-paper comparison set ([Mayo and Goldberg 2009](https://doi.org/10.2340/16501977-0343) [Abstract]). These count how often a label is right among self-labelled papers, not how often designs are mislabelled generally. A frequently repeated claim that 72% of cohort studies are mislabelled inverts the denominator: Esene's 71.87% is of papers titled case series ([Esene 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00381-014-2460-1) [Abstract]; [Mathes and Pieper 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5513097) [Direct, body text and tables; Figs 3-4 not seen], who state the claim citing Esene).

**Features before labels is authoritative guidance, not a tested benefit.** Cochrane prefers design features and asks reviewers to record what researchers did ([Handbook ch. 24](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-24) [Tool]; [Reeves 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5669452) [Tool]). Reeves was built by expert consensus, and I found no reliability test of it. AHRQ keeps labels as useful shorthand ([AHRQ report, p. 21](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]).

**No controlled evidence shows a flowchart helps anyone learn.** ERIC and Europe PMC searches found no study testing a flowchart, algorithm or decision tree for identifying epidemiological study designs ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/); [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/)). This is a better-searched absence, not proof of none: one ERIC query with an abstract field filter failed and was not re-run in that form, ERIC's medical-education coverage is partial, and Web of Science and MedEdPORTAL were not searched. The closest read study tests something else: case-based teaching of study design scored 72.81% combined, 69.70% case-based and 63.89% slides at final exam, n=234, but it compared different chapters, had no outer control, and its exam did not test design identification ([Shi 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1157420.pdf) [Full]). Its text also says "all P<0.001" where its Table 2 shows P=0.010 and 0.033. A randomised trial of a community-based epidemiology course was located and not read ([Zhang 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04787-z) [Not read; PMC closed in the third round]). The honest claim for the teaching value is narrow: structured tools have been revised and tested for reliability, and users asked for a visual step-by-step diagram with self-contained definitions, while two testers ignored the definitions altogether ([AHRQ report, pp. 12, 22](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). CDC's Exercise 1.7 is a small keyed classification task covering three of the six values ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct]), which makes it a usable test fixture and no evidence of learning.

## How to read the proposal

**Verification status** travels with every source: **[Full]** the full text was read directly; **[Tool]** the full text passed through a fetch summariser, so wording and locators are one step removed; **[Abstract]** abstract or record only; **[Second-hand]** known only through another read source; **[Metadata]** an index record only; **[Not read]**. Agency sources add **[Direct]**, meaning raw page or PDF text read with no summariser, for the passage and not the whole document unless marked, and **[Keyword]**, meaning a negative or locating result from a term search of the extracted text. Where two research notes gave one source different statuses, both are shown.

**Three status limits apply throughout.** First, German 2001 (MMWR 2001) and Buehler 2004 (MMWR 53(RR-5)) were read directly in the third round, so the earlier tag **[Not read; summariser report only]** is withdrawn. Both evaluate surveillance systems. Neither was written to say how a practitioner should classify a study, so what they support here is by application. Second, CDC's Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice, Self-Study Course SS1978, cited as SS1978 with lesson (L) and section, was read at archive.cdc.gov, under a banner saying it is archived and no longer updated, last reviewed 18 May 2012 (Lesson 6 Section 2, 15 September 2016). It is teaching material of that age, not current CDC guidance. Third, WHO Basic means Bonita, Beaglehole and Kjellström, Basic epidemiology, 2nd ed., WHO 2006; MOPECE means PAHO's Módulos de principios de epidemiología para el control de enfermedades, 3rd ed., 2017; ECDC DQ means ECDC's data quality and system evaluation handbook of September 2014. WHO page numbers are inferred from a PDF offset, one WHO table's cell order is unverified, and PAHO's Spanish is paraphrased here, so all need checking before quotation.

**Strength** is my grading, not a published scale. **Strong** means algebra or direct measurement read in full, with agreement across groups. **Moderate** means guidance or consensus read in full, or one measured study read in full. **Thin** means abstract-only, tool-only, one illustration, or disputed. **None** means no source bears on the node directly. Strength and status are separate: a Full reading of an opinion is still an opinion.

**Origin** states whether a question is adapted from a published instrument or is **Constructed**, meaning this report's wording, untested. The option wording in every table is constructed unless stated; only the JBI options and ROBINS codes exist in a published instrument.

**The stopping rule has two forms, and this is a design decision of this report.** At a design question (D1 to D5), "not sure" or "not stated" ends the path with no `design` value. At a later question (A, T and every refinement), the same answer leaves the declaration standing, withholds only the reading that depends on it, and records the open item. "Not sure" means the practitioner does not know; "not stated" means the source does not say. Knol's survey shows why this is a real branch rather than a courtesy: by my arithmetic from its Table 2, 35 of 125 incident-case studies (28%) were unclear on sampling time, source or method ([Knol 2008](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://boris.unibe.ch/27781/1/Knol%20AmJEpidemiol%202008.pdf) [Full]). AHRQ advises assuming a condition unmet when unclear, and ROBINS tools provide "No information" ([AHRQ report, p. 21](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]; [Higgins 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11098530/) [Full]). The evidence does not choose between them; stopping is chosen here because a guessed declaration would alter every downstream measure name.

Two sentences carry those stops, called N1 and N2 below. **N1:** `No study design was declared: ‹question› was answered "‹not sure | not stated›".` **N2:** `‹Question› was answered "‹not sure | not stated›", so ‹the dependent reading› is not given.` All other sentences extend the existing `studyDesignSentence` form and are shown to the practitioner before the choice is committed, the precedent set by the `relate_table` dialog. ‹Angle marks› are values the practitioner supplies.

## The design questions decide one of six or none

### D1. Did a standing system collect these counts to monitor occurrence?

**Evidence:** no classification tree read has a surveillance terminal, and no agency classification read lists surveillance as a study type. CDC's course lists it as a core epidemiologic task apart from field investigation and analytic studies ([SS1978 L1 §4](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section4.html) [Direct]). WHO's study-type table has no surveillance row ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 40-41](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and PAHO's classification has none ([MOPECE 5, pp. 5-7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]). WHO defines surveillance as ongoing systematic collection, analysis and interpretation ([WHO Basic, ch. 7, p. 127](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and CDC says most surveillance data suit descriptive methods ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]). STROBE asks authors to state original aims for secondary data ([STROBE E&E, item 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]). A system of low sensitivity can still follow trends if its sensitivity stays reasonably constant ([German 2001, Task D.2.e](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5013a1.htm) [Direct]). That now gives the caveat its CDC source, beside CDC's artefact list ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]) and ECDC's separation of system sensitivity from case-definition sensitivity ([ECDC DQ, ch. 1](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/portal/files/media/en/publications/Publications/Data-quality-monitoring-surveillance-system-evaluation-Sept-2014.pdf) [Direct]). German defines surveillance as an ongoing, systematic activity tied to public health action, and evaluates it as a system. Its Background lists uses that include evaluating public policy, forming research hypotheses and giving a basis for epidemiologic research, which fits D1's second option; it sets no conditions for those uses. **Strength:** None for the node, Moderate for the caveat, firmer. **Origin:** Constructed.

**D1 separates monitoring occurrence from comparing an exposure, and that is what handles WHO's overlap.** WHO's ecological definition includes one population at different times ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 41-43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), so the previous stated purpose of D1, keeping weekly area counts out of `ecological`, cannot rest on the unit of analysis: by WHO's definition such counts can be an ecological time series. The sources separate the two by purpose and by whether an exposure is compared. This is my reading, not a rule any source states. Counts read for occurrence stay at `surveillance`. The same counts read across an exposure change go to D2, reach `ecological`, and carry a T1 record. `surveillance` remains a Fieldwork declared value and the node's route; only the sentence changes.

|---|---|---|---|
| Yes, and the claim concerns occurrence over time | `surveillance`, then S | A trend is readable only while the way cases are found and reported stays stable; this declaration claims no study of an exposure | `Declared as surveillance data, ‹period›. Counts came from ‹system›, a standing system.` |
| Yes, but the records were used to compare exposures, including one population across an exposure change | D2 | Records were kept for another purpose. WHO's time-series ecological form lands here, with T1 recording the time structure | `Data came from ‹system›, collected for ‹original purpose›, not for this study.` |
| No, assembled for this question | D2 | None here | No sentence |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | Purpose decides whether a study claim is being made | N1 |

### D2. At what level are exposure and outcome known?

**Evidence:** group data give only marginal distributions, so the within-group joint distribution is unobserved ([Haneuse and Wakefield 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2802495) [Tool]; [Morgenstern 1995](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.pu.16.050195.000425) [Abstract]). Robinson's reversal reaches this report in two framings, and his own paper was [Not read]. Freedman gives .53 across 48 states against −.11 for individuals, for percent foreign-born and percent literate ([Freedman 1999, TR 549](https://statistics.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/tech-reports/549.pdf) [Full]). A re-analysis of the same census gives −0.53 across states and 0.12 for individuals, for illiteracy ([Subramanian 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2663721/) [Tool, first 100,000 of 119,486 characters]). The caveat needs the reversal and not the digits, so no figure goes in a note. That re-analysis also concludes the individualistic fallacy is a peril alongside the ecologic one, so a group result is not automatically the inferior one. **Strength:** None for the node, Moderate for the caveat, because the core methods papers were reached at abstract level or not at all. **Origin:** Constructed.

| Option | Route | Caveat carried | Machined note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both known per subject | D3 | State the subject, which may not be a person | No sentence beyond the existing unit clause |
| Both known only for groups | `ecological`, then E | A relation between groups need not hold between individuals | `Declared as an ecological study, ‹period›. Exposure and outcome were known only for each ‹group›.` |
| Outcome per subject, exposure taken from a group or place | D3 | Exposure is a proxy. Snow reports pump-wells inside company-supplied rural sub-districts ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]) | `Exposure was assigned from each subject's ‹group›, not measured on the subject.` |
| Exposure linked per case, denominator counted by group | D3 | Cases and denominator come from different sources and may differ in unit. No published treatment of this mixed form found. German's rule that numerators and denominators be comparable (Task D.2.g) applies by extension, which is my inference | `Exposure was linked to each ‹case›; the denominator was a count for each ‹group› from ‹source›.` |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | The unit decides what every measure means | N1 |

### D3. Did having the outcome decide who entered?

**Evidence:** "groups defined by outcome" appears across the trees read, including SIGN's final node ([SIGN](https://www.sign.ac.uk/assets/study_design.pdf) [Full]; [AHRQ App. G-H](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). CDC's course states the same boundary: cohort subjects are enrolled or grouped by exposure, case-control subjects by disease status ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct, whole section]). Its Lesson 6 retrospective cohort contacts every member of a defined population, which supports the "whole population" option ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). The audits above show why it matters, and Dekkers's sampling rule is reported by [Mathes and Pieper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5513097) and [JBI 2017](https://www.unisa.edu.au/contentassets/72bf75606a2b4abcaf7f17404af374ad/6i-jbi_critical_appraisal-checklist_for_case_series20171.pdf) (Dekkers [Not read, Second-hand]). **Strength:** Moderate: the node is widely shared and the need is measured, but placing it first is untested. **Origin:** Adapted from SIGN and AHRQ; opening on it departs from every algorithm tested for reliability.

|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | D4 | Having two groups does not make a case-control study; sampling on outcome does | Deferred to D4 |
| No, entry depended on exposure, membership or a sample drawn without regard to outcome | D5 | A survey later split by outcome is not case-control | `Subjects entered by ‹basis›, without regard to outcome.` |
| No, a whole population or register was taken | D5 | Coverage replaces sampling as the concern | `All ‹subjects› in ‹population› were included.` |

### D4. Were subjects without the outcome also sampled from the population the cases arose in?

**Evidence:** controls should generally reflect the population from which cases arose ([STROBE E&E, item 6a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full for that sentence]), and CDC and PAHO say the same, PAHO calling control selection the most critical aspect ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]; [MOPECE 5, p. 38](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]). An outcome-sampled series can be the case group of a case-control study and gives no risk ([Mathes and Pieper, Table 4 and text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5513097) [Direct]). "Comparisons of case series" were among the true designs of mislabelled surgical papers ([Mihailovic 2005](https://www.canjsurg.ca/content/48/2/148) [Tool]). **Strength:** Moderate. **Origin:** Adapted from SIGN and STROBE.

| Option | Route | Caveat carried | Machined note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | `case-control`, then CC | The odds ratio's target depends on control sampling | `Declared as a case-control study, ‹period›. Controls were sampled from ‹source population›.` |
| No, cases only | `case-series`, then CS | No risk and no relative measure | `Declared as a case-series study, ‹period›. Only subjects with the outcome were included.` |
| Yes, but the comparison subjects are patients with a different illness or strain | `case-control` by proposal (decision 10), then CC | A case-case study, which cannot measure disease risk (Table 7.1). Patients with similar illnesses can share exposures, which can hide the source, and patients from elsewhere may be a poor comparison ([Jackson and Griffin, Field Epidemiology Manual ch. 7](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/design-conduct-analyze-field-studies.html) [Direct, web page and supplied PDF]) | `Declared as a case-control study, ‹period›, of case-case form: the comparison subjects were patients with ‹other illness or strain›.` |
| A comparison group from elsewhere, such as another series or a historical group | Stop | A comparison of two series is not case-control and not one series | N1, naming the comparison group |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | Control source is often unclear in reports | N1 |

### D5. After entry, was each subject observed over time for the outcome to occur?

**Evidence:** every tree has a timing node, but placement and wording differ, and AHRQ's "gathered concurrently" drew disagreement on 2 of 15 studies ([AHRQ report, p. 13](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). The prospective/retrospective distinction stays out of the question, because STROBE documents three incompatible usages and testers often could not tell them apart ([STROBE E&E, item 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]; [AHRQ report, p. 12](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). **Strength:** Moderate for the node, Thin and disputed for the one-group option. **Origin:** Reworded from SIGN and AHRQ.

**"Retrospective cohort" returns as a printed term, not as a question.** It is CDC's field term: exposure and outcome have already happened, common for groups such as factory workers or wedding attendees, and in outbreaks every member of a defined population is contacted ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct]; [SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). Epidemiologists will expect it, so the note offers it with its meaning attached. The agency wording also exposes a trap, which is my inference: a wedding party interviewed once reports exposure and later illness in one sitting, and the previous option "measured at one time" would have sent it to `cross-sectional`. D5 now asks whether the data follow subjects across a period in which the outcome could occur, however the data were collected. CDC's Lesson 6 wording also sits loosely against its Lesson 1 summary, since one starts from a whole population and the other from exposure groups, which is another reason to print a definition.

| Yes, two or more exposure groups or levels | `cohort`, then C | Record dates; the follow-up may lie wholly in records or be reconstructed at one interview | `Declared as a cohort study, ‹period›. Subjects were observed from ‹start› for ‹outcome›.` If chosen, add: `Follow-up lay wholly in ‹records | interview recall›, which CDC's course calls a retrospective cohort.` |
| Yes, but everyone was exposed, so groups differ only by timing, place or dose | `cohort` by proposal (decision 11), then C | No unexposed group exists; CDC's remedy is to compare by exposure timing, place and dose ([Jackson and Griffin, Field Epidemiology Manual ch. 7](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/design-conduct-analyze-field-studies.html) [Direct, web page and supplied PDF]). CDC names no design for this | `Declared as a cohort study, ‹period›. Everyone was exposed, so ‹timing, place or dose› was compared.` |
| Yes, one exposure group, picked as cohort | `cohort`, then C | Absolute risk only, with no relative measure. Definition: sampling on exposure | `Declared as a cohort study without a comparison group, ‹period›, by the definition that sampling on exposure makes a cohort.` |

## Allocation is a separate record, and the label names its definition

**Allocation does not compete with cohort.** A cohort sample and a case-control sample can both sit on an allocation by a policy or a lottery, so a tree branch that substitutes "natural experiment" for "cohort" confuses two facts about a study. The closest precedent is Reeves's Q5, how groups were formed, which sits apart from the data-structure questions ([Reeves 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5669452) [Tool]). Rubin's assignment mechanism and the target-trial protocol also treat assignment as a component, not a design type ([Rubin 2008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0811.1640) [Full]; [Hernán and Robins 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4832051/) [Full/Tool]). CDC's course makes "investigator determines exposure" its first split, at individual or community level ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct]), which fits A1's first two options. No agency text read uses "natural experiment" as a design. Lesson 6's "experiment of nature" and the Manual's remark that almost all outbreaks are "natural experiments" ([Goodman, Buehler and Mott, Manual ch. 1](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/defining-field-epi.html) [Direct]) concern the opportunity an outbreak gives to learn, a different sense, and must not be cited for A1.

**The MRC line and Dunning make a substantive split, and no authority settles it.** The MRC line defines a natural experiment by who controls exposure: events or processes outside researchers' control that divide populations into exposure groups, with as-if randomisation explicitly not required and treated as one end of a spectrum ([Craig 2025, pp. 13, 16](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346937/1/346937.pdf) [Full]; [Craig 2012](https://www.ukri.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/NERC-301121-NaturalExperimentsGuidance.pdf) [Full]). Dunning defines it by the quality of assignment: random or as-if random assignment is the defining feature ([Dunning 2008, pp. 17-18](https://zenodo.org/records/996336/files/Dunning_2008_Natural_QMMR_6_2.pdf) [Full]; his 2012 book [Not read]). DiNardo sides broadly with the narrow reading ([DiNardo](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~jdinardo/ne7.pdf) [Full; publication details unverified]). Craig 2025 reports its broad definition as a compromise after some workshop support for the narrow one. Under Craig a selective policy rollout qualifies; under Dunning it does not. "Quasi-experiment" fares worse, with three meanings: any non-randomised design, a planned non-randomised experiment, or a synonym, so the tree should not print it ([Craig 2025, Table 3](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346937/1/346937.pdf) [Full]; [de Vocht 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7879679/) [Tool]).

**Neither side licenses causal language from the label.** Dunning calls balance on measured variables necessary if not sufficient, and proposes a continuum of plausibility. Craig 2025 says as-if randomisation cannot be fully determined by quantitative analysis and that a design label is an inadequate proxy for quality. I found no validated instrument or threshold for scoring as-if randomness; Dunning 2012 chapter 8 is the likely source and was [Not read]. The tree therefore records an argument, not a pass mark.

### T1. How is time structured?

**Before-and-after is neither sampling nor allocation.** DiD is a method: it handles fixed group differences and common shocks and does not require as-if random allocation. It rests on parallel counterfactual trends, no anticipation and no spillover ([Roth et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01194) [Full, arXiv v3]). Pre-trend tests are weak and can distort results ([Roth et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01194) [Full]; [Bilinski and Hatfield](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.03273v6) [Abstract]), and parallel trends cannot be tested ([Cunningham](https://mixtape.scunning.com/09-difference_in_differences) [Tool]). A control group can be worse than none if parallel trends fail ([Craig 2025](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346937/1/346937.pdf) [Full]). I found no DiD reporting guideline. CDC's course says secular trend graphs help officials evaluate programmes and infer what caused a change ([SS1978 L1 §6](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section6.html) [Direct]); that describes practice and is looser than this node, so it is no licence for causal wording. WHO's statement that confounding all but disappears in very short time series ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 41-43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]) must not enter a note: by my reading it concerns only slowly varying confounders. **Strength:** Moderate. **Origin:** Constructed; the three-points rule comes from AHRQ's tree.

### Cohort

**C1, the three dates.** Alignment of eligibility, assignment and start of follow-up is the root of immortal-time and prevalent-user bias ([Hernán 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5124536/) [Full]; [Hernán and Robins 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4832051/) [Full/Tool]). A structural account shows immortal time arising from eligibility applied after assignment or from strategies classified with later information, gives a ratio of about 0.67 under a true null in a 16-person teaching example, and adds that person-time analysis avoids immortal time without aligning eligibility and assignment, depending on a constant hazard ratio the authors call rarely plausible ([Hernán et al., Epidemiology 36(1):107-114](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11598638/) [Tool]). It supports the mechanism and the fix. Suissa's growth of bias with immortal duration stays [Abstract] ([Suissa 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwm324)), and Lévesque's hazard ratios stay barred. ROBINS-I V2 items 2.1, 2.2, 3.1, 3.2 map here ([launch version, 22 Nov 2024](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1u9CiAQZ8OBFW29RLVdMsQnzy6qSsCZUG/export?format=pdf) [Full for Domains 2-3]; a later release is unverified). **Strength:** Moderate, firmer than before. **C2, follow-up and population.** Closed cohorts can estimate risks and rates, and open cohorts estimate rates ([STROBE E&E, Box 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]). A single hazard ratio depends on follow-up length ([Hernán 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3653612/) [Tool]). STROBE and Vandenbroucke and Pearce use closed, open, fixed and dynamic differently, so the question avoids those words ([Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2012a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465772/) [Full/Tool]). **Strength:** Moderate. **C3, loss.** A simulation found no important bias at 5% to 60% loss under MCAR or MAR and serious bias at low loss under MNAR ([Kristman 2004](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:EJEP.0000036568.02655.f8) [Abstract]), so the tree offers no percentage threshold. **Strength:** Thin. **C4, new or prevalent exposure.** The bias is well described ([Ray 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwg231) [Abstract]), and its necessity is disputed ([Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4634310/) [abstract read closely]; [Hernán 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4634308/) [Full, partial]), though the gap is narrower than the titles suggest, because Hernán 2016 accepts prevalent users for stopping and switching. **Strength:** Thin and disputed.

### Case-control

**The estimand is the strongest evidence in the whole tree, and Knol's figure is already a published tree.** It runs case type, source population, sampling design, assumptions ([Knol 2008, Fig. 1](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://boris.unibe.ch/27781/1/Knol%20AmJEpidemiol%202008.pdf) [Full]). The algebra is confirmed across [Labrecque 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7850067/) [Full], [Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2012b](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://academic.oup.com/ije/article-pdf/41/5/1480/2106653/dys147.pdf) [Full] and [Kerr 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10502767/) [Full]; [Pearce 1993](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/22.6.1189), [Greenland and Thomas 1982](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a113439) and [Miettinen 1976](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a112220) were [Abstract]. **Strength:** Strong for the mapping, though the authors overlap, so this is not convergence of independent groups, and nearly all of it is algebra or illustration, not comparison in real data. **Origin:** Adapted from Knol's Figure 1, adding Kerr's constant-hazard caveat.

**Two points are live disputes, and the sentences stay on the side both can accept.** Labrecque says density-sampled studies must report rate ratios; Kerr, with Greenland as coauthor, calls that directive inaccurate, because a time-matched odds ratio reads as a rate ratio only under further conditions. Pearce, Knol and STROBE take the middle position, reporting the odds ratio and stating what it estimates. A rule of "state the reading and the assumption" satisfies all but Labrecque's "must"; it is my synthesis. Knol classes time-matched studies as needing no further assumption, and Kerr requires a constant hazard ratio, failing which the odds ratio targets a weighted mean of hazard ratios. Practical size looks modest: in Kerr's deliberately large example the rate ratio was 3.34, the matched odds ratio 3.02 and the unmatched 2.61, and Kerr calls the differences not dramatic. Those are illustrations, not empirical error rates. The rare disease question appears only on the survivor branch, since Knol found it discussed in four studies, none of which needed it, while none of the studies needing a stable population discussed that. No validated rarity threshold exists: under 5% is Vandenbroucke and Pearce's gloss and the Manual's rough example, and 10% and 20% are Second-hand through Knol.

**CDC's current field chapter backs the control rules and the licence.** Controls should reflect the source population and be chosen independently of the exposure under evaluation, overmatching should be avoided, and two control groups that agree add credibility. It says a risk ratio cannot be calculated from a case-control study, and that an odds ratio can approximate a risk ratio when the disease is rare, roughly under 5%, running typically higher above that ([Jackson and Griffin, Field Epidemiology Manual ch. 7](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/design-conduct-analyze-field-studies.html) [Direct, web page and supplied PDF]). It says nothing on density or case-cohort sampling, and neither does the Manual's analysis chapter, so CC3 still rests on the other sources. That chapter says neither attack rates nor a risk ratio can be calculated from a case-control study and that the analysis must account for matching ([Dicker, Manual ch. 8](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/analyze-interpret-data.html) [Direct]).

**The audience's training wording is simpler, and CC3 must be explained against it.** CDC's course says the odds ratio approximates the relative risk for a rare disease and that most outbreak-associated diseases count as rare, without sampling distinctions ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). PAHO notes the same ad/bc arithmetic gives an odds ratio in cohort and cross-sectional data ([MOPECE 5, p. 38](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]). This is not a contradiction: CDC's statement is the survivor row of CC3. Case-control sampling variants did not appear in the agency passages read, so no agency position on CC3's other options is established. CC6 is added because CC1 to CC5 recorded timing and matching and not where controls came from. Its options are CDC's four named kinds, and its origin is Constructed.

### Cross-sectional

**Evidence:** CDC's course rates cross-sectional analytically weaker than cohort or case-control because it usually cannot separate risk factors for incidence from risk factors for survival, while calling it a fine descriptive tool ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct, whole section]), which gives X1 and X2 agency footing. STROBE calls temporality especially difficult here, while allowing that a congenital exposure precedes disease ([STROBE E&E, Box 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]); limits are possible rather than universal ([Savitz and Wellenius 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwac037) [Abstract]). Prevalent-case bias rests on a compartment model of case-control studies, applied to surveys here by extension ([Hill 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0895-4356(02)00571-1) [Abstract]). Length bias rests on one dementia cohort, with survival 6.6 years unadjusted against 3.3 adjusted ([Wolfson 2001](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM200104123441501) [Abstract]). On the ratio, Thompson and Barros and Hirakata prefer the prevalence ratio, Zocchetti leaves it to epidemiological grounds, and STROBE is neutral, while the Manual calls both commonly used and the prevalence ratio easier to explain ([Thompson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1136/oem.55.4.272) [Abstract; full text refused, PMC closed in the third round]; [Barros and Hirakata 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC521200/) [Full]; [Zocchetti 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/26.1.220) [Abstract]; [Dicker, Manual ch. 8](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/analyze-interpret-data.html) [Direct]). By my algebra a prevalence ratio of 2.0 gives a prevalence odds ratio of 2.11 at 5% baseline and 3.50 at 30%. Design effects come from STROBE item 12d, which does not discuss sampling weights. The Manual adds one sentence, that cross-sectional data might need weights or design effects in the analysis (ch. 8, Planning the Analysis); survey-statistics primary texts were not read. **Strength:** Moderate for X1, Moderate for X2 on one agency passage, Moderate for X3 on the arithmetic and disputed on the choice, Thin for X4. **Origin:** all Constructed; neither ROBINS tool has a cross-sectional variant, and I found no validated signalling questions.

### Ecological

**Evidence:** WHO is now the best agency source here. It defines the design by unit, says such studies usually use data collected for other purposes and cannot make the individual-level link, and rates ecological evidence the weakest for causation because of extrapolation to individuals ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 40-43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]; [WHO Basic, ch. 5, Table 5.3, pp. 95-96](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]). Morgenstern separates inference about individuals from inference about group rates, and warns against conflating etiologic and programme evaluation ([Morgenstern 1995](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.pu.16.050195.000425), [Morgenstern 1982](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.72.12.1336), both [Abstract]). A group target can be legitimate, as for a congestion charge ([Loney and Nagelkerke 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1742-7622-11-18) [Full/Tool]). Idrovo's three-part test is a letter, not a validated tool ([Idrovo 2011](https://doi.org/10.1289/ehp.1103768) [Full]). Loney and Nagelkerke's reassurance that non-linearity mostly alters magnitude conflicts in tone with [Greenland and Robins 1994](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a117069) [Abstract], and should not be repeated until the latter is read. Freedman's ecological-regression example put high-income share at 29% and 85% against truth of 35% and 28% ([Freedman 1999, TR 549](https://statistics.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/tech-reports/549.pdf) [Full]). Individual-level odds ratios also move across model specifications, for example foreign-born white 7.31 single-level against 13.63 with state effects ([Subramanian 2009, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2663721/) [Tool]). **Strength:** Thin overall: Morgenstern 1995, Greenland and Robins 1994, Greenland 2001, Piantadosi 1988 and Wakefield 2008 were not read in full. **Origin:** all Constructed; no source provides a question set.

**E1 can no longer say that every individual claim is only a lead.** WHO grades ecological evidence weak and then states exceptions: exposures not normally measurable per person, such as air pollution or fluoride, make it very important; time series can quantify effects already established; and asthma deaths rising and falling with bronchodilator availability is offered as good evidence for causation ([WHO Basic, ch. 5, pp. 95-96](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]). Loney and Nagelkerke add an instrument-style route: a group variable can support a claim about individuals when it is correlated with exposure, unconfounded with the outcome, acts only through exposure, and is monotonic ([Loney and Nagelkerke 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4350299/) [Full/Tool]). E1 gains three options for these, and each records a stated reason and keeps the withheld individual ratio. The bronchodilator example is one textbook illustration, so it supports the option's existence and no automatic upgrade. PAHO files ecological among hypothesis-evaluating studies and WHO calls it useful for generating them ([MOPECE 5, pp. 5-7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]; [WHO Basic, ch. 3, p. 43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and that disagreement is carried forward.

**E6 is added, Constructed and Thin.** The branch had no question on whether the outcome was recorded alike across groups, although S1 asks exactly that over time. The direct source is a weak one: an undated UK learning page, not an agency document, which lists area differences in coding, diagnosis and reporting completeness ([HealthKnowledge](https://www.healthknowledge.org.uk/index.php/e-learning/epidemiology/practitioners/introduction-study-design-gs) [Direct]). The same page also files health surveillance as a type of geographical study, so it is used for this point alone. ECDC's rationale that standard case definitions allow comparison across populations, places and times supports it by extension ([ECDC DQ, §1.3.2, p. 9](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/portal/files/media/en/publications/Publications/Data-quality-monitoring-surveillance-system-evaluation-Sept-2014.pdf) [Direct]).

### Case series

**Evidence:** WHO describes a case series as patient characteristics not compared with a reference population, often prompting fuller study ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, p. 39](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and PAHO files it as descriptive ([MOPECE 5, pp. 5-7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]); CDC's course does not use the term ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Keyword]). Case definitions change as knowledge grows, as SARS did during 2003 ([SS1978 L1 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section5.html) [Direct]), which bears on CS1. The Manual gives a definition three parts, a clinical or laboratory condition, a period and a setting, and expects it to be revised and narrowed ([King et al., Manual ch. 3, Step 4](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/field-investigation.html) [Direct]), so CS1 should date its definition. Beyond that, definitions conflict three ways. Dekkers samples on outcome (reported by JBI and Mathes and Pieper; Dekkers [Not read]). GRADE's glossary describes patients usually all receiving one intervention ([GRADE Handbook](https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html) [Tool]). Murad says the uncontrolled nature and small size define a series, not sampling ([Murad 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjebm-2017-110853) [Full]). JBI's ten items and Murad's eight are the published instruments, and JBI's options Yes / No / Unclear / Not applicable are source wording ([JBI 2017](https://www.unisa.edu.au/contentassets/72bf75606a2b4abcaf7f17404af374ad/6i-jbi_critical_appraisal-checklist_for_case_series20171.pdf) [Full]). Murad advises against an aggregate score. Series give proportions and event rates, not odds ratios or relative risks; Murad's "event rates" hold only with a defined denominator, which is my reconciliation. **Strength:** Moderate for CS1 to CS3 as guidance, Thin for CS4, with no agreement data on the JBI and Murad tools found. **Origin:** CS1 and CS2 adapted from JBI items 1 to 5; CS3 Constructed; CS4 adapted from Murad's causal items.

### Surveillance

**Evidence:** CDC's Lesson 5 is the best agency list of artefacts: passive to active reporting, case definition change, publicity, more diagnosis, new tests, physician awareness, a similar disease misdiagnosed, laboratory error, batch reporting, population growth and duplicates, with resort and college towns adding sudden numerator shifts ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]; [SS1978 L6 §2, Step 2](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). Its figures show a true salmonellosis rise and a definition-driven AIDS rise looking alike. It says rates are preferred and a denominator is a critical prior step, that saying incidence rose requires comparison over time or across areas, and that the increase warranting action is set locally, which supports refusing numeric thresholds ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]). **It separates acting from claiming:** officials usually treat an apparent increase as real and respond until proven otherwise ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]). German also names heightened awareness, new diagnostic tests and changed surveillance methods as causes of changed sensitivity ([German 2001, Task D.2.e](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5013a1.htm) [Direct]), and the Manual requires spurious rises, called pseudoepidemics, to be ruled out before an outbreak is confirmed ([King et al., Manual ch. 3, Step 3](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/field-investigation.html) [Direct]). I found no CDC text in the sources read stating when surveillance data support an analytic or causal claim: German lists evaluation among the uses and gives no conditions, and ch. 8's causal discussion is about analytic studies.

Measured cases back this up. Seven COVID-19 definition versions changed the detected proportion by 7.1-fold, 2.8-fold and 4.2-fold, with about 232,000 cases estimated under version 5 against 55,508 reported, from a simple exponential model the authors say cannot separate interventions and testing capacity ([Tsang 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(20)30089-X) [Full]). England's move to episodes added about 4.0%, or 588,114 cases ([UKHSA blog](https://ukhsa.blog.gov.uk/2022/02/04/changing-the-covid-19-case-definition/) [Direct]), and UKHSA flags testing policy, a step change in influenza ascertainment and a system migration as trend caveats ([UKHSA DQ](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sources-of-surveillance-data-for-influenza-covid-19-and-other-respiratory-viruses/data-quality-report-national-flu-and-covid-19-surveillance-report) [Direct]). Its change log is a model for dated revisions ([UKHSA HCAI QMI](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/hcai-qmi-report/quality-and-methodology-information-qmi-for-healthcare-associated-infections-hcai-reports) [Direct]). Completeness cannot be borrowed: multipliers ran 0.4 to 2,082.9 for salmonellosis and 0.4 to 39,000 for campylobacteriosis, and capture-recapture corrects underreporting and not under-ascertainment ([Gibbons 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4015559/) [Tool, first 100,000 of 177,160 characters]). Delayed death reporting, with a 3-week delay and 2-week doubling time, understates case fatality about 2.8-fold, and preferential detection of severe cases inflates it ([Lipsitch 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4504518/) [Tool]). Lipsitch and Gibbons address case fatality and foodborne multipliers, so applying them to trend claims in S2 and S4 is by extension. Right truncation hides recent cases ([McGough 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007735) [Full]). A statistical signal prompts checks and does not define an outbreak ([Noufaily 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/bty997) [Full]; [SS1978 L6 §2, Step 2](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). Buehler adds that a statistical deviation from baseline can trigger investigation and is not enough to define an outbreak, which a jurisdiction defines by whether occurrence has changed enough to warrant attention ([Buehler 2004, B.2, Validity](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5305a1.htm) [Direct]); that framework concerns system evaluation, and its support for S5 is by application. **This corrects S3.** German says denominators often come from a separate system, and asks that numerators and denominators be comparable across categories and that the denominator's source stay consistent when measuring trends ([German 2001, Task D.2.g](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5013a1.htm) [Direct]). The earlier options treated any other source as approximate, which would have marked ordinary census denominators down; they now turn on comparability. German also calls the share of unknown or blank responses a simple measure of data quality and says a full assessment may need a special study (Task D.2.c), which agrees with S2's blanks option. For S7, the Manual offers the period just before or the same period a year earlier, and warns of varying definitions and incomplete reporting ([King et al., Manual ch. 3, Step 3](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/field-investigation.html) [Direct]). For S6, it prefers onset, notes surveillance systems may have only a report date, and sets epidemic-curve intervals at half an incubation or latency period or less, shrinking as cases rise ([Fontaine, Manual ch. 6](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/describing-epi-data.html) [Direct]). The claim that bin choice changes visible peaks stays unconfirmed: the Manual gives the interval rule without saying so, Lesson 6's figures show one outbreak at different intervals, and only the captions were read ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 6](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct, captions]). **Strength:** Moderate for S1 and S4, with firmer support than before; Moderate for S2 on the caveat that completeness cannot be borrowed; Moderate for S3, its comparability rule now read in German; Moderate for S5, firmer; Moderate for S6 on the Manual's date and interval guidance, and Thin on what bin choice does to a curve; Moderate for S7 on two agency passages. **Origin:** all Constructed. I found no signalling tool for surveillance-based inference, and GRADE was silent in the portions read.

## South London shows the tree without a seventh design

**`Deaths per house, by water company` keeps `cohort` only as a stated reading.** Snow says house supply was fixed by the owner or occupier's decision when companies competed, so people did choose; the argument is that the choice predated the 1852 intake move and was unrelated to the later contamination contrast, and "assigned at random" would be wrong ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]; [Dunning 2008, p. 18](https://zenodo.org/records/996336/files/Dunning_2008_Natural_QMMR_6_2.pdf) [Full]). The research found no authority calling this a cohort, and the term "cohort study" was proposed in 1960 ([Morabia 2001](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.1007/bf01318790/full) [Full]). The comparison has cohort logic, but exposure was looked up only for deaths, and the denominator is a company count from another source. Three further limits matter. The 40,046 and 26,107 are whole-company counts spanning sub-districts served by one company, so the as-if argument covers less than the 315 against 37 headline; I saw no read source make this point. The row is a `supplier`, and `deaths` over `houses` is not a proportion of houses, as the workflow's own `rateKind:"ratio"` concedes, so the licence sentence "a/(a+b) is a risk" does not describe it. And the ratio moves with period and denominator: 8.40 on seven-week houses by my recomputation, about 14 for four weeks by Snow's rounded rates, about 6 for the whole epidemic per population, and about 3.5 on Simon's linked figures, which Snow attributes to address errors ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]; [Snow 1856](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66507) [Full/Tool]). Vandenbroucke notes that Snow's second edition plays down part of the data by emphasising the first part of the epidemic ([Vandenbroucke 2001](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.1007/bf01321079/full) [Full]). A note would state:

`Declared as a cohort study, 1854. The unit of analysis is the house. Exposure was linked to each death; the denominator was a count of houses for each supplier from a return to Parliament. Exposure was allocated outside the investigator's control by the companies' supply, argued to be unrelated to other causes of the outcome because the choice of supply predated the 1852 intake move. This is an argument and not a demonstration, and it applies to the intermixed sub-districts.`

**`Died or survived, by water supply` is the cleanest case.** Companies attach to sub-districts, so the design is `ecological`, and Snow himself treats the area table as a prompt for the more decisive intermixed test. His question was about individuals, so E1 applies and the result is a lead, not an individual effect. Freedman calls the area comparisons ecological and says Snow was careful to avoid the fallacy ([Freedman 1999, TR 521](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ed260/freedman521.pdf) [Full]). The README's 5.35 on people and 300,149 excluded people in the both-companies stratum are carried into E4:

`Declared as an ecological study, 1854. The unit of analysis is the sub-district. The claim concerns individuals, and these data are group-level, so a relation between groups is reported as a lead and not as an individual effect. Sub-districts supplied by both companies were excluded; they were not controlled for.`

**`Cholera in 1849 compared with 1854` needs no new design.** The draft treated it as a missing design. Under three records it is `ecological`, with allocation by the Lambeth Company's move and a before-and-after time record. Summing the file gives `deaths_1849` to `deaths_1854` of 2,261 to 2,458 for `Southwark and Vauxhall Company only` (1854 deaths per 1849 death 1.09) and 3,905 to 2,547 for `Lambeth Company and Southwark and Vauxhall Company` (0.65). For `Lambeth Company only` the research note gives 162 to 37 (0.23), while the file leaves `deaths_1854` blank for Dulwich, so summing only complete rows gives 161; I did not run the workflow to see which it shows. These are counts, deliberately, because the repository holds one population. Cunningham reads a modified Table XII as a precursor to DiD ([Cunningham](https://mixtape.scunning.com/09-difference_in_differences) [Tool]), and that is a modern reinterpretation, not Snow's analysis, so it should not be presented as a DiD estimate. Snow himself flags that Lambeth's supply extended to Streatham, Norwood and Sydenham after 1849 and that Streatham's 1849 count reflected an asylum outbreak ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]), which compromises exactly the group that carries the contrast. Whether the sub-district comparison meets Dunning's narrow definition is doubtful in my reading, since his as-if argument is about intermixed houses. It meets the MRC line's broad one. So the README's sentence that the move upstream "makes this a natural experiment" is true under one definition and should name it.

`Declared as an ecological study, 1849 and 1854. The unit of analysis is the sub-district. Exposure was allocated outside the investigator's control by the Lambeth Company's move of its intake in 1852. deaths_1849 and deaths_1854 were compared against sub-districts whose supply did not change. These are counts, and no rate or difference-in-differences estimate is given. This is a natural experiment in the broad sense, exposure being outside the investigator's control (Craig et al. 2025). It is not thereby a natural experiment in the narrow sense, which requires random or as-if random assignment (Dunning 2008).`

## What CDC's field chapter changed

[Jackson and Griffin, Field Epidemiology Manual ch. 7](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/design-conduct-analyze-field-studies.html) [Direct, web page and supplied PDF]. Published as chapter 7, updated 8 August 2024. The web page added what the PDF clipped, including Table 7.1's case-case column.

- **A third field design.** The chapter names cohort, case-control and case-case as the types used most in the field. D4 had no route for case-case; it now has one, subject to decision 10.
- **Retrospective cohort confirmed.** A defined group enrolled after the outcome, such as banquet attendees or a school's students, is a cohort, and the potluck example interviews everyone once. That confirms D5's guard against sending it to `cross-sectional`.
- **Its choice rule is about planning.** A well-defined population points to a cohort, and a poorly defined one to case-control. The tree classifies a study that was done, so this is an aside for planning and not a branch.
- **Allocation.** In observational studies the epidemiologist documents rather than determines exposures, which supports A1's investigator option. The chapter does not discuss natural experiments.
- **Matching.** Firmer than Pearce on a matched analysis, as CC4 now states.
- **Case-case measure.** Table 7.1 says case-case cannot measure disease risk, which corrects this note's statement that the chapter gives no measure rule. D4's caveat and decision 10 now carry it.
- **Outside the tree so far.** Analytic studies should typically test hypotheses and not generate them, and exposure should be defined objectively and applied consistently. Neither has a question here yet.

## Conclusion

Adding open evidence changed the proposal's grounding more than its shape, and the most useful result is where agency wording exposed a defect that the academic literature did not. The D5 trap and the sentence "Declared as a surveillance study" would both have been noticed first by an epidemiologist in the room, and both are small fixes. CDC's separation of acting from claiming is the idea worth teaching: a practitioner can respond to an increase and decline to assert it, and Fieldwork can record both. The 2026-11-06 subset grows slightly in preparation, not in scope. D1's reworded sentence, D5's rewording and CC3's explanation against CDC's rare-disease wording matter there, and the rest of this update can wait.

The honest educational claim is now sharper: the tree teaches by exposing disputes and consequences, and no study shows that works, for this tree or for any flowchart found.
