# Ask three questions before naming designs

_Created 2026-11-01 · Updated 2026-11-01_

**Standing of this note.** A proposal, and nothing in it is built. It replaces the first draft of this file, which carried a wrong Labrecque citation, a contested claim that density-sampled studies must report rate ratios, and natural experiment as a branch. Every source carries how it was read, and unread sources stay marked unread.

Ground the tree on three separate records, not one branching label: how subjects were sampled and observed (which yields one of Fieldwork's six declared values), who or what allocated exposure, and how time is structured. Open agency sources now back most of the design nodes and caveats, because CDC's Principles of Epidemiology course, WHO Basic Epidemiology and PAHO's MOPECE were read directly, and they separate designs by comparison group, who determines exposure, enrolment and timing. Their largest correction falls on the surveillance terminal: **no agency classification read lists surveillance as a study type**, so the note must stop saying "Declared as a surveillance study", and that sentence is one template string in `fieldwork/src/study-design.ts`. **Chapter 7 of the CDC Field Epidemiology Manual adds a third field design this tree could not route (below). German 2001 (MMWR 2001), Buehler 2004 and Manual chapters 1, 3, 6 and 8 have now been read directly, and they add no surveillance study type. None of the 15 named gated papers was read in full, and in the fourth round PMC again raised a check, this time before any of the listed PDFs arrived**, so the proposal claims nothing from them. The closed citations that remained have been replaced, softened or removed, and the fourth round's test of CDC's Exercise 1.7 sent a randomised trial to `cohort`, which A1 now stops. The evidence remains strong for the case-control estimand algebra, moderate for cohort alignment, reporting guidance and surveillance artefacts, and thin for question order and the ecological branch. **No agency source read offers a decision tree ending in these six values, and ERIC and Europe PMC searches found no study testing a flowchart for design identification**, so the educational claim is a reasoned design and not a tested benefit. Nothing here has been built or tested with practitioners.

## Seven findings set the shape of the tree

**Published trees share a small question set, but none covers Fieldwork's six designs.** AHRQ's round 2 tree, DASHCI, the American Dietetic Association tool, RTI-UNC and SIGN all draw on comparison, assignment, randomisation, group count, timing and how groups were defined ([AHRQ report](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]; [SIGN](https://www.sign.ac.uk/assets/study_design.pdf) [Full]). "Ecological" appears nowhere in the AHRQ report, "surveillance" appears only for a rejected drug-surveillance tool, and the ADA tool routes "correlational" to noncomparative ([AHRQ report](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). Dekkers 2012 was not read, so it appears below only as Mathes and Pieper and JBI report it. Grimes and Schulz 2002 was not read and is not used.

**Agency texts give axes and prose rules, not a tree.** CDC's course calls a comparison group "the key feature of analytic epidemiology", and separates experimental from observational by whether the investigator determines exposure, then observational designs by entry and timing ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct, whole section]). The closest thing to a rule is one sentence of outbreak advice: a retrospective cohort in a small, well-defined population with a full list, case-control when the population is not well defined and speed matters ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). PAHO gives a classification tree without questions ([MOPECE 5, Figure 5.1, p. 7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]), and WHO a suitability grid whose cell values could not be recovered from the extraction ([WHO Basic, Table 3.3, p. 49](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct, columns only]). This holds for everything read, including Manual chapter 7, which gives a prose choice rule and a benefits table (Table 7.1) and no tree. Chapters 1, 3, 6 and 8 add none either. **The agency texts also do not agree on terminals:** CDC names cohort, case-control and cross-sectional, with no ecological design and no use of the term case series ([SS1978 L1 §3](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section3.html) [Keyword]). WHO and PAHO name ecological and case series, and only PAHO files ecological as analytic.

**No head-to-head study shows which question order is best.** AHRQ users preferred opening with comparison because assignment copes poorly with mixed methods, and that is a stated preference, not a tested effect ([AHRQ report, p. 22](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]).

**Agreement is low on hard tasks and higher on narrow ones.** AHRQ measured Fleiss κ=0.26 with 13 categories on 30 purposively difficult studies, 0.33 among its reference raters, and 0.45 on a random 15 after revision, a rise that cannot be credited to the tool alone because the sample halved and half the testers changed ([AHRQ report, Table 4](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). A detailed-instruction tool reached κ=0.67 ([Seo 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.09.013) [Abstract]). A binary "true case series or not" call reached 100% between two raters on 64 papers ([Esene 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00381-014-2460-1) [Abstract]). That contrast supports short binary nodes, though no study tests six categories, and all of this measures reviewers reading papers, not practitioners describing their own study.

**The label audits show which boundary leaks most.** Self-labelled case-control papers were judged not case-control in 65% of 55 surgical papers ([Mihailovic 2005](https://www.canjsurg.ca/content/48/2/148) [Tool]), 67% of 339 orthopaedic papers ([LeBrun 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11420-020-09753-9) [Abstract]), 70.7% of 386 ophthalmology papers, of which 190 of 273 were cross-sectional, about 70% by arithmetic ([2026 audit](https://doi.org/10.1167/tvst.15.4.11) [Tool; its erratum not read]), and 97% of 86 rehabilitation papers against 34% of a 221-paper comparison set ([Mayo and Goldberg 2009](https://doi.org/10.2340/16501977-0343) [Abstract]). These count how often a label is right among self-labelled papers, not how often designs are mislabelled generally. A frequently repeated claim that 72% of cohort studies are mislabelled inverts the denominator: Esene's 71.87% is of papers titled case series ([Esene 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00381-014-2460-1) [Abstract]; [Mathes and Pieper 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5513097) [Direct, body text and tables; Figs 3-4 not seen], who state the claim citing Esene).

**Features before labels is authoritative guidance, not a tested benefit.** Cochrane prefers design features and asks reviewers to record what researchers did ([Handbook ch. 24](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-24) [Tool]; [Reeves 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5669452) [Tool]). Reeves was built by expert consensus, and I found no reliability test of it. AHRQ keeps labels as useful shorthand ([AHRQ report, p. 21](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]).

**No controlled evidence shows a flowchart helps anyone learn.** ERIC and Europe PMC searches found no study testing a flowchart, algorithm or decision tree for identifying epidemiological study designs ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/); [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/)). This is a better-searched absence, not proof of none: one ERIC query with an abstract field filter failed and was not re-run in that form, ERIC's medical-education coverage is partial, and Web of Science and MedEdPORTAL were not searched. The closest read study tests something else: case-based teaching of study design scored 72.81% combined, 69.70% case-based and 63.89% slides at final exam, n=234, but it compared different chapters, had no outer control, and its exam did not test design identification ([Shi 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1157420.pdf) [Full]). Its text also says "all P<0.001" where its Table 2 shows P=0.010 and 0.033. A trial of community cohort practice does not change this ([Zhang 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04787-z) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). Among 75 undergraduates, 41 had theory plus practice and 34 theory alone. Self-rated understanding (p=0.0126) and clinical practice competency (p=0.0005) rose, while test scores did not differ clearly: 78.88 against 75.76, p=0.0859, adjusted β 1.738, p=0.511. Allocation is called random, but the groups match the summer and autumn terms, baseline differed, only a cohort study was practised, and design identification was not tested. It weakly supports practice raising self-rated skill, not measured knowledge. Pearce recommends teaching epidemiology through real problems, which is an editorial position and not a test ([Pearce 2000](https://doi.org/10.1136/jech.54.5.326) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). The honest claim for the teaching value is narrow: structured tools have been revised and tested for reliability, and users asked for a visual step-by-step diagram with self-contained definitions, while two testers ignored the definitions altogether ([AHRQ report, pp. 12, 22](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). CDC's Exercise 1.7 is a small keyed classification task covering three of the six values and one randomised trial ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct]; [answers page](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/exerciseanswers.html) [Direct]), which makes it a usable test fixture and no evidence of learning. Its hand walk through the tree is in the final section.

## How to read the proposal

**Verification status** travels with every source: **[Full]** the full text was read directly; **[Tool]** the full text passed through a fetch summariser, so wording and locators are one step removed; **[Abstract]** abstract or record only; **[Second-hand]** known only through another read source; **[Metadata]** an index record only; **[Not read]**. Agency sources add **[Direct]**, meaning raw page or PDF text read with no summariser, for the passage and not the whole document unless marked, and **[Keyword]**, meaning a negative or locating result from a term search of the extracted text. Where two research notes gave one source different statuses, both are shown.

**Three status limits apply throughout.** First, German 2001 (MMWR 2001) and Buehler 2004 (MMWR 53(RR-5)) were read directly in the third round, so the earlier tag **[Not read; summariser report only]** is withdrawn. Both evaluate surveillance systems. Neither was written to say how a practitioner should classify a study, so what they support here is by application. Second, CDC's Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice, Self-Study Course SS1978, cited as SS1978 with lesson (L) and section, was read at archive.cdc.gov, under a banner saying it is archived and no longer updated, last reviewed 18 May 2012 (Lesson 6 Section 2, 15 September 2016). It is teaching material of that age, not current CDC guidance. Third, WHO Basic means Bonita, Beaglehole and Kjellström, Basic epidemiology, 2nd ed., WHO 2006; MOPECE means PAHO's Módulos de principios de epidemiología para el control de enfermedades, 3rd ed., 2017; ECDC DQ means ECDC's data quality and system evaluation handbook of September 2014. WHO page numbers are inferred from a PDF offset, one WHO table's cell order is unverified, and PAHO's Spanish is paraphrased here, so all need checking before quotation.

**Strength** is my grading, not a published scale. **Strong** means algebra or direct measurement read in full, with agreement across groups. **Moderate** means guidance or consensus read in full, or one measured study read in full. **Thin** means abstract-only, tool-only, one illustration, or disputed. **None** means no source bears on the node directly. Strength and status are separate: a Full reading of an opinion is still an opinion.

**Origin** states whether a question is adapted from a published instrument or is **Constructed**, meaning this report's wording, untested. The option wording in every table is constructed unless stated; only the JBI options and ROBINS codes exist in a published instrument.

**The stopping rule has two forms, and this is a design decision of this report.** At a design question (D1 to D5), "not sure" or "not stated" ends the path with no `design` value. At a later question (A, T and every refinement), the same answer leaves the declaration standing, withholds only the reading that depends on it, and records the open item. "Not sure" means the practitioner does not know; "not stated" means the source does not say. Knol's survey shows why this is a real branch rather than a courtesy: by my arithmetic from its Table 2, 35 of 125 incident-case studies (28%) were unclear on sampling time, source or method ([Knol 2008](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://boris.unibe.ch/27781/1/Knol%20AmJEpidemiol%202008.pdf) [Full]). AHRQ advises assuming a condition unmet when unclear, and ROBINS tools provide "No information" ([AHRQ report, p. 21](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]; [Higgins 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11098530/) [Full]). The evidence does not choose between them; stopping is chosen here because a guessed declaration would alter every downstream measure name.

Two sentences carry those stops, called N1 and N2 below. **N1:** `No study design was declared: ‹question› was answered "‹not sure | not stated›".` **N2:** `‹Question› was answered "‹not sure | not stated›", so ‹the dependent reading› is not given.` All other sentences extend the existing `studyDesignSentence` form and are shown to the practitioner before the choice is committed, the precedent set by the `relate_table` dialog. ‹Angle marks› are values the practitioner supplies.

## The design questions decide one of six or none

### D1. Did a standing system collect these counts to monitor occurrence?

**Evidence:** no classification tree read has a surveillance terminal, and no agency classification read lists surveillance as a study type. CDC's course lists it as a core epidemiologic task apart from field investigation and analytic studies ([SS1978 L1 §4](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section4.html) [Direct]). WHO's study-type table has no surveillance row ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 40-41](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and PAHO's classification has none ([MOPECE 5, pp. 5-7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]). WHO defines surveillance as ongoing systematic collection, analysis and interpretation ([WHO Basic, ch. 7, p. 127](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and CDC says most surveillance data suit descriptive methods ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]). STROBE asks authors to state original aims for secondary data ([STROBE E&E, item 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]). A system of low sensitivity can still follow trends if its sensitivity stays reasonably constant ([German 2001, Task D.2.e](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5013a1.htm) [Direct]). That now gives the caveat its CDC source, beside CDC's artefact list ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]) and ECDC's separation of system sensitivity from case-definition sensitivity ([ECDC DQ, ch. 1](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/portal/files/media/en/publications/Publications/Data-quality-monitoring-surveillance-system-evaluation-Sept-2014.pdf) [Direct]). German defines surveillance as an ongoing, systematic activity tied to public health action, and evaluates it as a system. Its Background lists uses that include evaluating public policy, forming research hypotheses and giving a basis for epidemiologic research, which fits D1's second option; it sets no conditions for those uses. **Strength:** None for the node, Moderate for the caveat, firmer. **Origin:** Constructed.

**D1 separates monitoring occurrence from comparing an exposure, and that is what handles WHO's overlap.** WHO's ecological definition includes one population at different times ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 41-43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), so the previous stated purpose of D1, keeping weekly area counts out of `ecological`, cannot rest on the unit of analysis: by WHO's definition such counts can be an ecological time series. The sources separate the two by purpose and by whether an exposure is compared. This is my reading, not a rule any source states. Counts read for occurrence stay at `surveillance`. The same counts read across an exposure change go to D2, reach `ecological`, and carry a T1 record. `surveillance` remains a Fieldwork declared value and the node's route; only the sentence changes.

|---|---|---|---|
| Yes, and the claim concerns occurrence over time | `surveillance`, then S | A trend is readable only while the way cases are found and reported stays stable; this declaration claims no study of an exposure | `Declared as surveillance data, ‹period›. Counts came from ‹system›, a standing system.` |
| Yes, but the records were used to compare exposures, including one population across an exposure change | D2 | Records were kept for another purpose. WHO's time-series ecological form lands here, with T1 recording the time structure | `Data came from ‹system›, collected for ‹original purpose›, not for this study.` |
| No, assembled for this question | D2 | None here | No sentence |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | Purpose decides whether a study claim is being made | N1 |

### D2. At what level are exposure and outcome known?

**Evidence:** group data give only marginal distributions, so the within-group joint distribution is unobserved ([Haneuse and Wakefield 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2802495) [Tool]; [Morgenstern 1982](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.72.12.1336) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], Table 2 and p. 1337, which calls this the key feature of ecologic data). Robinson's reversal reaches this report in two framings, and Robinson's own paper was [Not read]. Freedman gives .53 across 48 states against −.11 for individuals, for percent foreign-born and percent literate ([Freedman 1999, TR 549](https://statistics.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/tech-reports/549.pdf) [Full]). A re-analysis of the same census gives −0.53 across states and 0.12 for individuals, for illiteracy ([Subramanian 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2663721/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). The caveat needs the reversal and not the digits, so no figure goes in a note. That re-analysis also concludes the individualistic fallacy is a peril alongside the ecologic one, so a group result is not automatically the inferior one. **Strength:** None for the node, Moderate for the caveat, which rests on Morgenstern 1982 and open worked examples. The later ecological-bias methods papers are closed and carry none of it. **Origin:** Constructed.

| Option | Route | Caveat carried | Machined note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both known per subject | D3 | State the subject, which may not be a person | No sentence beyond the existing unit clause |
| Both known only for groups | `ecological`, then E | A relation between groups need not hold between individuals | `Declared as an ecological study, ‹period›. Exposure and outcome were known only for each ‹group›.` |
| Outcome known by group, no exposure measured | `ecological`, then E | Morgenstern's exploratory study: geographic patterns suggest hypotheses and test none ([Morgenstern 1982](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.72.12.1336) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], p. 1337) | `Declared as an ecological study, ‹period›. Rates of ‹outcome› were compared across ‹groups›; no exposure was measured, so this suggests hypotheses and tests none.` |
| Outcome per subject, exposure taken from a group or place | D3 | Not an ecological study: this is the multilevel cell of [Subramanian et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyn359) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], Fig. 5. Say whether the group value is a proxy for each subject's exposure, or a true property of the place, such as a law. As a proxy it can misclassify: Snow reports pump-wells inside company-supplied rural sub-districts ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]) | `Exposure was assigned from each subject's ‹group›, not measured on the subject.` |
| Exposure linked per case, denominator counted by group | D3 | Cases and denominator come from different sources and may differ in unit. No published treatment of this mixed form found. German's rule that numerators and denominators be comparable (Task D.2.g) applies by extension, which is my inference | `Exposure was linked to each ‹case›; the denominator was a count for each ‹group› from ‹source›.` |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | The unit decides what every measure means | N1 |

### D3. Did having the outcome decide who entered?

**Evidence:** "groups defined by outcome" appears across the trees read, including SIGN's final node ([SIGN](https://www.sign.ac.uk/assets/study_design.pdf) [Full]; [AHRQ App. G-H](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). CDC's course states the same boundary: cohort subjects are enrolled or grouped by exposure, case-control subjects by disease status ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct, whole section]). Its Lesson 6 retrospective cohort contacts every member of a defined population, which supports the "whole population" option ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). The audits above show why it matters, and Dekkers's sampling rule is reported by [Mathes and Pieper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5513097) and [JBI 2017](https://www.unisa.edu.au/contentassets/72bf75606a2b4abcaf7f17404af374ad/6i-jbi_critical_appraisal-checklist_for_case_series20171.pdf) (Dekkers [Not read, Second-hand]). **Strength:** Moderate: the node is widely shared and the need is measured, but placing it first is untested. **Origin:** Adapted from SIGN and AHRQ; opening on it departs from every algorithm tested for reliability.

| Option | Route | Caveat carried | Machined note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | D4 | Having two groups does not make a case-control study; sampling on outcome does | Deferred to D4 |
| No, entry depended on exposure, membership or a sample drawn without regard to outcome | D5 | A survey later split by outcome is not case-control | `Subjects entered by ‹basis›, without regard to outcome.` |
| No, a whole population or register was taken | D5 | Coverage replaces sampling as the concern | `All ‹subjects› in ‹population› were included.` |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | This is the boundary the audits find failing most | N1 |

### D4. Were subjects without the outcome also sampled from the population the cases arose in?

**Evidence:** controls should generally reflect the population from which cases arose ([STROBE E&E, item 6a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full for that sentence]), and CDC and PAHO say the same, PAHO calling control selection the most critical aspect ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]; [MOPECE 5, p. 38](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]). An outcome-sampled series can be the case group of a case-control study and gives no risk ([Mathes and Pieper, Table 4 and text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5513097) [Direct]). "Comparisons of case series" were among the true designs of mislabelled surgical papers ([Mihailovic 2005](https://www.canjsurg.ca/content/48/2/148) [Tool]). **Strength:** Moderate. **Origin:** Adapted from SIGN and STROBE.

| Option | Route | Caveat carried | Machined note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | `case-control`, then CC | The odds ratio's target depends on control sampling | `Declared as a case-control study, ‹period›. Controls were sampled from ‹source population›.` |
| No, cases only | `case-series`, then CS | No risk and no relative measure | `Declared as a case-series study, ‹period›. Only subjects with the outcome were included.` |
| Yes, but the comparison subjects are patients with a different illness or strain | `case-control` by proposal (decision 10), then CC | A case-case study, which cannot measure disease risk (Table 7.1). Patients with similar illnesses can share exposures, which can hide the source, and patients from elsewhere may be a poor comparison ([Jackson and Griffin, Field Epidemiology Manual ch. 7](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/design-conduct-analyze-field-studies.html) [Direct, web page and supplied PDF]) | `Declared as a case-control study, ‹period›, of case-case form: the comparison subjects were patients with ‹other illness or strain›.` |
| A comparison group from elsewhere, such as another series or a historical group | Stop | A comparison of two series is not case-control and not one series | N1, naming the comparison group |
| Not sure / not stated | Stop | Control source is often unclear in reports | N1 |

### D5. After entry, was each subject observed over time for the outcome to occur?

**Evidence:** every tree has a timing node, but placement and wording differ, and AHRQ's "gathered concurrently" drew disagreement on 2 of 15 studies ([AHRQ report, p. 13](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). The prospective/retrospective distinction stays out of the question, because STROBE documents three incompatible usages and testers often could not tell them apart ([STROBE E&E, item 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]; [AHRQ report, p. 12](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/study-design-classification_research.pdf) [Full]). **Strength:** Moderate for the node, Thin and disputed for the one-group option. **Origin:** Reworded from SIGN and AHRQ.

**"Retrospective cohort" returns as a printed term, not as a question.** It is CDC's field term: exposure and outcome have already happened, common for groups such as factory workers or wedding attendees, and in outbreaks every member of a defined population is contacted ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct]; [SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). Epidemiologists will expect it, so the note offers it with its meaning attached. D5 now asks whether the data follow subjects across a period in which the outcome could occur, however the data were collected. CDC's Lesson 6 wording also sits loosely against its Lesson 1 summary, since one starts from a whole population and the other from exposure groups, which is another reason to print a definition.

## Allocation is a separate record, and the label names its definition

**Allocation does not compete with cohort.** A cohort sample and a case-control sample can both sit on an allocation by a policy or a lottery, so a tree branch that substitutes "natural experiment" for "cohort" confuses two facts about a study. The closest precedent is Reeves's Q5, how groups were formed, which sits apart from the data-structure questions ([Reeves 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5669452) [Tool]). Rubin's assignment mechanism and the target-trial protocol also treat assignment as a component, not a design type ([Rubin 2008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0811.1640) [Full]; [Hernán and Robins 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4832051/) [Full/Tool]). CDC's course makes "investigator determines exposure" its first split, at individual or community level ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct]), which fits A1's first two options. No agency text read uses "natural experiment" as a design. Lesson 6's "experiment of nature" and the Manual's remark that almost all outbreaks are "natural experiments" ([Goodman, Buehler and Mott, Manual ch. 1](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/defining-field-epi.html) [Direct]) concern the opportunity an outbreak gives to learn, a different sense, and must not be cited for A1.

**The MRC line and Dunning make a substantive split, and no authority settles it.** The MRC line defines a natural experiment by who controls exposure: events or processes outside researchers' control that divide populations into exposure groups, with as-if randomisation explicitly not required and treated as one end of a spectrum ([Craig 2025, pp. 13, 16](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346937/1/346937.pdf) [Full]; [Craig 2012](https://www.ukri.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/NERC-301121-NaturalExperimentsGuidance.pdf) [Full]). Dunning defines it by the quality of assignment: random or as-if random assignment is the defining feature ([Dunning 2008, pp. 17-18](https://zenodo.org/records/996336/files/Dunning_2008_Natural_QMMR_6_2.pdf) [Full]; Dunning's 2012 book [Not read]). DiNardo sides broadly with the narrow reading ([DiNardo](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~jdinardo/ne7.pdf) [Full; publication details unverified]). Craig 2025 reports its broad definition as a compromise after some workshop support for the narrow one. Under Craig a selective policy rollout qualifies; under Dunning it does not. "Quasi-experiment" fares worse, with three meanings: any non-randomised design, a planned non-randomised experiment, or a synonym, so the tree should not print it ([Craig 2025, Table 3](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346937/1/346937.pdf) [Full]; [de Vocht 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7879679/) [Tool]).

**Susser gives a third definition, and names Snow.** Natural experiments here are sharp, well-defined events of distinct magnitude, preferably with pre-existing data, such as the Dutch famine or the Bangladesh flood ([Susser 1994, part II](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.84.5.830) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). Snow's water supply study is called a well-demarcated natural experiment, on the evidence of intimately mingled households. So three definitions now stand: who controlled exposure, how good the assignment was, and how sharp the event was. This strengthens decision 4, since the label cannot be printed without its definition. It is one more use of the term and does not settle what the label licenses.

**Neither side licenses causal language from the label.** Dunning calls balance on measured variables necessary if not sufficient, and proposes a continuum of plausibility. Craig 2025 says as-if randomisation cannot be fully determined by quantitative analysis and that a design label is an inadequate proxy for quality. I found no validated instrument or threshold for scoring as-if randomness; Dunning 2012 chapter 8 is the likely source and was [Not read]. The tree therefore records an argument, not a pass mark.

### T1. How is time structured?

**Before-and-after is neither sampling nor allocation.** DiD is a method: it handles fixed group differences and common shocks and does not require as-if random allocation. It rests on parallel counterfactual trends, no anticipation and no spillover ([Roth et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01194) [Full, arXiv v3]). Pre-trend tests are weak and can distort results ([Roth et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01194) [Full]; [Bilinski and Hatfield](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.03273v6) [Abstract]), and parallel trends cannot be tested ([Cunningham](https://mixtape.scunning.com/09-difference_in_differences) [Tool]). A control group can be worse than none if parallel trends fail ([Craig 2025](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346937/1/346937.pdf) [Full]). I found no DiD reporting guideline. CDC's course says secular trend graphs help officials evaluate programmes and infer what caused a change ([SS1978 L1 §6](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section6.html) [Direct]); that describes practice and is looser than this node, so it is no licence for causal wording. WHO's statement that confounding all but disappears in very short time series ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 41-43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]) must not enter a note: by my reading it concerns only slowly varying confounders. **Strength:** Moderate. **Origin:** Constructed; the three-points rule comes from AHRQ's tree.

### Cohort

**C1, the three dates.** Alignment of eligibility, assignment and start of follow-up is the root of immortal-time and prevalent-user bias ([Hernán 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5124536/) [Full]; [Hernán and Robins 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4832051/) [Full/Tool]). A structural account shows immortal time arising from selection on eligibility applied after assignment, or from misclassification when assignment uses information after eligibility, gives a ratio of about 0.67 under a true null in a 16-person teaching example, and adds that person-time analysis avoids immortal time without aligning eligibility and assignment, depending on a constant hazard ratio the authors call rarely plausible ([Hernán et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001808) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]; Epidemiology 2025;36(1):107-114, online 4 Nov 2024). The authors call the bias a consequence of incorrect analysis and not of observational data, and say alignment at time zero prevents it. Alignment is impossible where data are left-truncated or hold cases and controls only; the options are then to change the question or use cloning with censoring or the g-formula. Landmark and person-time analyses do not remove the misalignment. It supports the mechanism and the fix. It does not say how bias grows with the length of immortal time: that claim was Suissa 2008's, a closed paper, so the tree no longer makes it, and no open source read fills the gap. Lévesque's hazard ratios stay barred. ROBINS-I V2 items 2.1, 2.2, 3.1, 3.2 map here ([launch version, 22 Nov 2024](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1u9CiAQZ8OBFW29RLVdMsQnzy6qSsCZUG/export?format=pdf) [Full for Domains 2-3]; a later release is unverified). **Strength:** Moderate, firmer than before. **C2, follow-up and population.** Closed cohorts can estimate risks and rates, and open cohorts estimate rates ([STROBE E&E, Box 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]). A single hazard ratio depends on follow-up length ([Hernán 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3653612/) [Tool]). STROBE and Vandenbroucke and Pearce use closed, open, fixed and dynamic differently, so the question avoids those words ([Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2012a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465772/) [Full/Tool]). **Strength:** Moderate. **C3, loss.** A simulation found no important bias at 5% to 60% loss under MCAR or MAR and serious bias at low loss under MNAR ([Kristman 2004](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:EJEP.0000036568.02655.f8) [Abstract]), so the tree offers no percentage threshold. **Strength:** Thin. **C4, new or prevalent exposure.** The bias is well described ([Ray 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwg231) [Abstract]), and its necessity is disputed ([Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4634310/) [abstract read closely]; [Hernán 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4634308/) [Full, partial]), though the gap is narrower than the titles suggest, because Hernán 2016 accepts prevalent users for stopping and switching. **Strength:** Thin and disputed.

### Case-control

**The estimand is the strongest evidence in the whole tree, and Knol's figure is already a published tree.** It runs case type, source population, sampling design, assumptions ([Knol 2008, Fig. 1](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://boris.unibe.ch/27781/1/Knol%20AmJEpidemiol%202008.pdf) [Full]). The algebra is confirmed across [Labrecque 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7850067/) [Full], [Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2012b](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://academic.oup.com/ije/article-pdf/41/5/1480/2106653/dys147.pdf) [Full] and [Kerr 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10502767/) [Full]. The older closed papers behind it, Pearce 1993, Greenland and Thomas 1982 and Miettinen 1976, were seen only as abstracts and no longer carry any part of it. **Strength:** Strong for the mapping, though the authors overlap, so this is not convergence of independent groups, and nearly all of it is algebra or illustration, not comparison in real data. **Origin:** Adapted from Knol's Figure 1, adding Kerr's constant-hazard caveat.

**Two points are live disputes, and the sentences stay on the side both can accept.** Labrecque says density-sampled studies must report rate ratios; Kerr, with Greenland as coauthor, calls that directive inaccurate, because a time-matched odds ratio reads as a rate ratio only under further conditions. Knol and STROBE take the middle position, reporting the odds ratio and stating what it estimates. A rule of "state the reading and the assumption" satisfies all but Labrecque's "must"; it is my synthesis. Knol classes time-matched studies as needing no further assumption, and Kerr requires a constant hazard ratio, failing which the odds ratio targets a weighted mean of hazard ratios. Practical size looks modest: in Kerr's deliberately large example the rate ratio was 3.34, the matched odds ratio 3.02 and the unmatched 2.61, and Kerr calls the differences not dramatic. Those are illustrations, not empirical error rates. The rare disease question appears only on the survivor branch, since Knol found it discussed in four studies, none of which needed it, while none of the studies needing a stable population discussed that. No validated rarity threshold exists: under 5% is Vandenbroucke and Pearce's gloss and the Manual's rough example, and 10% and 20% are Second-hand through Knol.

**CDC's current field chapter backs the control rules and the licence.** Controls should reflect the source population and be chosen independently of the exposure under evaluation, overmatching should be avoided, and two control groups that agree add credibility. It says a risk ratio cannot be calculated from a case-control study, and that an odds ratio can approximate a risk ratio when the disease is rare, roughly under 5%, running typically higher above that ([Jackson and Griffin, Field Epidemiology Manual ch. 7](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/design-conduct-analyze-field-studies.html) [Direct, web page and supplied PDF]). It says nothing on density or case-cohort sampling, and neither does the Manual's analysis chapter, so CC3 still rests on the other sources. That chapter says neither attack rates nor a risk ratio can be calculated from a case-control study and that the analysis must account for matching ([Dicker, Manual ch. 8](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/analyze-interpret-data.html) [Direct]).

**The audience's training wording is simpler, and CC3 must be explained against it.** CDC's course says the odds ratio approximates the relative risk for a rare disease and that most outbreak-associated diseases count as rare, without sampling distinctions ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 8](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). PAHO notes the same ad/bc arithmetic gives an odds ratio in cohort and cross-sectional data ([MOPECE 5, p. 38](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]). This is not a contradiction: CDC's statement is the survivor row of CC3. Case-control sampling variants did not appear in the agency passages read, so no agency position on CC3's other options is established. CC6 is added because CC1 to CC5 recorded timing and matching and not where controls came from. Its options are CDC's four named kinds, and its origin is Constructed.

### Cross-sectional

**Evidence:** CDC's course rates cross-sectional analytically weaker than cohort or case-control because it usually cannot separate risk factors for incidence from risk factors for survival, while calling it a fine descriptive tool ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Direct, whole section]), which gives X1 and X2 agency footing. STROBE calls temporality especially difficult here, while allowing that a congenital exposure precedes disease ([STROBE E&E, Box 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2020496/) [Full]); limits are possible rather than universal ([Savitz and Wellenius 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwac037) [Abstract]). Prevalent-case bias rests on a compartment model of case-control studies, applied to surveys here by extension ([Hill 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0895-4356(02)00571-1) [Abstract]). Length bias rests on one dementia cohort, with survival 6.6 years unadjusted against 3.3 adjusted ([Wolfson 2001](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM200104123441501) [Abstract]). On the ratio, the case for the prevalence ratio is now read in full, along with the case against it. **For the prevalence ratio:** it is easier to interpret and communicate ([Lee and Chia 1993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1061320/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]; [Lee and Chia 1994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128127/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]; [Lee and Chia 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128364/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]), the prevalence odds ratio equals an incidence density ratio only if disease duration is similar in exposed and unexposed, which is seldom checked ([Axelson 1994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128040/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]), and a third factor can move the prevalence odds ratio while leaving the prevalence ratio alone: crude 2.83 against adjusted 3.00, with the prevalence ratio 2.14 both ways ([Axelson 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128270/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). [Thompson 1998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1757577/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] gives the fullest case: in a modelled dynamic cohort the prevalence ratio stays below the incidence rate ratio, while the prevalence odds ratio can land either side depending on follow-up. Thompson concludes that the prevalence ratio should be preferred, and calling it a prevalence ratio is honest labelling, because everyone knows prevalence is a weaker basis for causal inference. For purely descriptive work the authors say the prevalence odds ratio should not be used. Lee and Chia agree that prevalence is what planning needs ([Lee and Chia 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128364/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). **For the prevalence odds ratio:** [Strömberg 1994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1127925/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] shows that prevalence odds equal incidence times duration under stationarity, so the ratio converts to an incidence rate ratio given the duration ratio, which the prevalence ratio cannot do. [Strömberg 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128170/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] rejects the claim that it lacks meaning, since it is simply a ratio of prevalence odds, and shows that the confounding point cuts both ways: a factor can modify the prevalence ratio and leave the prevalence odds ratio unchanged (2.00 and 2.50 against 3.00 and 3.00). The correct reading of Axelson's example is modification and not confounding, by a factor unrelated to exposure. Thompson answers that stationarity and equal duration are doubtful, as people who become ill often leave exposure.

**"Conservative" does not mean safe.** Thompson's Table 1 includes a true incidence rate ratio of 5 with a prevalence ratio of 0.9 and a prevalence odds ratio of 0.8 at 50 years, when the exposed leave ten times faster. Thompson's formulas reproduce that row, recomputed here. So the prevalence ratio sits below the incidence rate ratio, but it can sit on the wrong side of 1. This is a reason for X2's question, and it is why no ratio from cross-sectional data is offered as an incidence measure. The model assumes one exposure, constant rates, chronic irreversible disease, and leavers replaced by disease-free people. It is a deterministic model and has no empirical test. **Do not copy Thompson's printed intervals:** recomputed from the counts, Table 3's stratum 2 odds ratio interval is 2.6 to 8.9 and not 1.3 to 5.5, and Table 5's stratum 1 interval is 4.6 to 7.8 and not 3.2 to 14.4. The point estimates and fitted coefficients do reproduce.

**This matters most where Fieldwork is meant to go.** Thompson says prevalence surveys may be the only choice in developing countries, where data systems are scarce, and that aetiological work with cross-sectional designs is typical in less developed settings. So X3 will be met often by the practitioners this project is for.

**How to adjust is a second dispute.** Lee and Chia use Breslow's constant-follow-up form of the Cox model, and Strömberg objects that constant follow-up has no clear meaning in a cross-sectional study. They reply that it is assumed only for mathematical convenience, and that its Poisson error makes standard errors too large when prevalence is high ([Lee and Chia 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1128364/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). Strömberg's claim that no confidence interval exists for a prevalence ratio from logistic regression is answered by Thompson's Appendix 2, which gives the variance. Thompson lists logistic regression with that variance, log-binomial models which may need constraints, and proportional hazards as conservative. Later work on robust Poisson is listed under disputes below. Barros and Hirakata say the odds ratio itself is correct, and the common error is to read it as a risk, and the Manual calls both commonly used with the prevalence ratio easier to explain ([Barros and Hirakata 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC521200/) [Full]; [Dicker, Manual ch. 8](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/analyze-interpret-data.html) [Direct]). STROBE is neutral. Zocchetti 1997 and Lee 1994 are paywalled and unread. By my algebra a prevalence ratio of 2.0 gives a prevalence odds ratio of 2.11 at 5% baseline and 3.50 at 30%. Design effects come from STROBE item 12d, which does not discuss sampling weights. The Manual adds one sentence, that cross-sectional data might need weights or design effects in the analysis (ch. 8, Planning the Analysis); survey-statistics primary texts were not read. **Strength:** Moderate for X1, Moderate for X2 on one agency passage, Moderate for X3 on the arithmetic; on the choice, the sources lean to the prevalence ratio, with a real dissent on interpretation, and the case rests on letters and one deterministic model; Thin for X4; Thin for X5, which is Constructed from one disagreement. **Origin:** all Constructed; neither ROBINS tool has a cross-sectional variant, and I found no validated signalling questions.

### Ecological

**Evidence:** WHO is now the best agency source here. It defines the design by unit, says such studies usually use data collected for other purposes and cannot make the individual-level link, and rates ecological evidence the weakest for causation because of extrapolation to individuals ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, pp. 40-43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]; [WHO Basic, ch. 5, Table 5.3, pp. 95-96](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]). Morgenstern separates testing an etiologic hypothesis from evaluating a population intervention, where the inference is about groups and not individuals ([Morgenstern 1982](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.72.12.1336) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], p. 1342). A group target can be legitimate, as for a congestion charge ([Loney and Nagelkerke 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1742-7622-11-18) [Full/Tool]). Idrovo's three-part test is a letter, not a validated tool ([Idrovo 2011](https://doi.org/10.1289/ehp.1103768) [Full]). Loney and Nagelkerke's reassurance that this bias rarely creates a false association should not be repeated: Morgenstern's hypothetical groups show a risk ratio of 4.33 ecologically and 1.00 within every group (Tables 3-4). That example is confounding by group and not non-linearity, so setting it against the reassurance is my reading. Whether non-linearity alone can create an association is a gap, since the open sources read do not settle it. Freedman's ecological-regression example put high-income share at 29% and 85% against truth of 35% and 28% ([Freedman 1999, TR 549](https://statistics.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/tech-reports/549.pdf) [Full]). Individual-level odds ratios also move across model specifications, for example foreign-born white 7.31 single-level against 13.63 with state effects ([Subramanian 2009, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2663721/) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). **Strength:** Thin overall: the ecological-bias algebra is in closed papers that were not read, so the tree says group data can mislead and shows open worked examples. **Origin:** all Constructed; no source provides a question set.

**Morgenstern's four types fit the tree without a new question.** They are exploratory (no exposure measured), multiple-group comparison, time trend in one population, and mixed: change in exposure against change in rate across several groups ([Morgenstern 1982](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.72.12.1336) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], pp. 1337-1339). So D2 and T1 together already identify the type: one period across groups is a comparison, before-and-after in one population is a time trend, and before-and-after across groups is mixed. The paper says the mixed design generally tests an etiologic hypothesis better than the other ecologic designs, being less open to confounding by extraneous risk factors (p. 1339). Morgenstern gives no tree, and the mapping onto D2 and T1 is this report's.

**Cross-level bias has a stated condition and a worked example.** It is the sum of aggregation bias, from grouping, and specification bias, the confounding effect of the group. There is none only when the group mean of the exposure has no effect on individual risk, given the individual's own exposure (pp. 1339-1340). A hypothetical five groups give a risk ratio of 4.33 ecologically, 1.50 crude and 1.00 within every group (Tables 3-4, recomputed here and confirmed). Durkheim's data give 7.57 ecologically against about 2 at individual level (p. 1339). Morgenstern says cross-level bias ordinarily exaggerates the association, though it can run either way in theory (p. 1340). The paper also recommends regression over correlation, since grouping inflates the correlation (p. 1342), which is a further reason no correlation goes into a machined note. Other limits named are that disease can influence group exposure, predictors are more correlated at group level, and smaller units bring more migration and less stable rates.

**The target of inference comes first, and both directions can fail.** Ecological units can legitimately be the target, while aggregate analysis alone cannot separate what a place does from who lives there. Individual analysis fails too: in the same 1930 data, conditioning on state moved the odds ratio for foreign-born whites from 7.31 to 13.63 and for blacks from 11.66 to 5.86, reversing their order ([Subramanian et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyn359) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], Table 2). That paper is a critique arguing for multilevel analysis, and it does not question Robinson's finding that ecological correlations cannot substitute for individual ones. Its Fig. 5 gives the four-cell typology of outcome level by exposure level, which supports D2's options directly.

**Morgenstern and Schwartz disagree, and the tree records the choice rather than settling it.** Schwartz's argument is that the ecological fallacy is a problem of construct validity: a group variable may measure something different from its individual namesake, as neighbourhood poverty differs from personal poverty. Schwartz objects to three assumptions: that individual models are better specified, that ecological studies only substitute for individual ones, and that only individual characteristics cause disease ([Schwartz 1994](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.84.5.819) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). Schwartz says Morgenstern treats contextual effects only as confounders, not as causes (p. 820, note), and that Morgenstern's alternative explanation for Durkheim, minority status, could only be tested by contextual analysis (p. 822). Schwartz also notes that Durkheim used individual data alongside the group data (p. 822). The two agree that ecological studies usually cannot substitute for individual ones, and that group data can sometimes be more accurate, such as sales records against reported drinking. Hypothesis generation as the only use is, in Schwartz's view, not generally supportable, though it holds where the discrepancy is confounding alone and the confounders are unknown (p. 820). E8 is built from this; its wording is constructed. Note that this is a position paper. The same journal issue also carried Susser's second paper on design, Koopman and Longini, and an editorial by Poole, none read here.

**Susser supplies the vocabulary E8 uses, and corrects the word "bias".** [Susser 1994](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.84.5.825) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] defines integral and contextual variables, and defines the fallacy as assuming that an association at one level can be inferred from another, in either direction; going from individuals up is the atomistic fallacy (pp. 828-829). Susser calls aggregation and specification effects, and says "bias" is improper unless individuals are the sole concern (p. 828). That is a real difference from Morgenstern's terms, so notes should say the group and individual results differ, and name a bias only once the target is stated. Susser's Table 1 gives the same four cells as Subramanian's Fig. 5, with one disagreement: Subramanian calls group outcome with individual exposure impossible to specify, while Susser gives an example, vaccinated persons affecting prevalence (p. 828). In theory the size of group and individual coefficients cannot be ordered, though in practice group ones are commonly larger (p. 828), which sits close to Morgenstern's claim of exaggeration. **Do not use Fig. 4 as printed:** an erratum (Am J Public Health 1995;85:1063) reports its panels reversed and the label bw wrong, and the corrected version should be read instead.

**The names need care, and a fourfold list of fallacies helps.** "Contextual variable" means different things across these papers: Susser uses it for what [Diez-Roux 1998](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.88.2.216) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] calls a derived variable, and Diez-Roux avoids the term. So E8 uses derived and integral, and keeps "contextual" for the analysis. Diez-Roux gives four fallacies (Table 2, p. 219). Ecological: group data, individual inference. Atomistic: individual data, group inference. Psychologistic: individual data, relevant group variables left out. Sociologistic: group data, relevant individual variables left out. "Individualistic fallacy" is used for more than one of these, so a note should not use it. The first pair is solved by matching data to the level of inference; the second is the harder problem, since one level may not explain a process alone (p. 219). Diez-Roux adds three cautions (pp. 219-220). A group effect can vanish when individual variables on its causal path are adjusted for, and that does not make them the true cause; statistics alone cannot settle which it is. The contextual unit must be chosen and defended, as neighbourhoods rarely match census tracts. And outcomes within a group may be correlated, which breaks the assumption of independent observations. Multilevel analysis is not always needed (p. 220). This is a Research Forum essay arguing for multilevel work.

**The fallacy is older and wider than ecology.** It is Aristotle's fallacy of division: a hung jury is indecisive, and its members are anything but (p. 821). It also occurs in individual studies, as when education stands in for diet, or when a vaccine trial's result is applied to why one vaccinated person stayed well (p. 821). That supports D2's proxy caveat and gives the notes a plain teaching example.

**E1 can no longer say that every individual claim is only a lead.** WHO grades ecological evidence weak and then states exceptions: exposures not normally measurable per person, such as air pollution or fluoride, make it very important; time series can quantify effects already established; and asthma deaths rising and falling with bronchodilator availability is offered as good evidence for causation ([WHO Basic, ch. 5, pp. 95-96](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]). Loney and Nagelkerke add an instrument-style route: a group variable can support a claim about individuals when it is correlated with exposure, unconfounded with the outcome, acts only through exposure, and is monotonic ([Loney and Nagelkerke 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4350299/) [Full/Tool]). E1 gains three options for these, and each records a stated reason and keeps the withheld individual ratio. The bronchodilator example is one textbook illustration, so it supports the option's existence and no automatic upgrade. PAHO files ecological among hypothesis-evaluating studies and WHO calls it useful for generating them ([MOPECE 5, pp. 5-7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]; [WHO Basic, ch. 3, p. 43](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and that disagreement is carried forward.

**Susser's second paper says when group data are the right tool.** It sets four levels of fit: obligate and apt, optional and apt, optional and not apt but convenient, and maladroit ([Susser 1994, part II](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.84.5.830) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner]). Dependent happenings such as infection make ecological analysis obligate, which supports G1. In group experiments the group is the unit that sets power. Six rules follow: have enough groups, account for integral and contextual effects, prefer small groups, keep groups alike apart from the study variables, assemble groups around the exposure, and with few groups stratify in advance. These support E3, E4 and E7. They are one author's advice and carry no test, and the levels of fit are too coarse to be a question, so they remain caveats. [Pearce 2000](https://doi.org/10.1136/jech.54.5.326) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] adds that many risk factors do not vary enough within a population to study there, and some act only at population level, which supports E1's group-target options and E8's integral option. It is an editorial, and it says "individualistic fallacy", the term barred above. Blakely and Woodward 2000, in the same journal issue, is paywalled and unread.

**E6 is added, Constructed and Thin.** The branch had no question on whether the outcome was recorded alike across groups, although S1 asks exactly that over time. The direct source is a weak one: an undated UK learning page, not an agency document, which lists area differences in coding, diagnosis and reporting completeness ([HealthKnowledge](https://www.healthknowledge.org.uk/index.php/e-learning/epidemiology/practitioners/introduction-study-design-gs) [Direct]). The same page also files health surveillance as a type of geographical study, so it is used for this point alone. ECDC's rationale that standard case definitions allow comparison across populations, places and times supports it by extension ([ECDC DQ, §1.3.2, p. 9](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/portal/files/media/en/publications/Publications/Data-quality-monitoring-surveillance-system-evaluation-Sept-2014.pdf) [Direct]).

### Case series

**Evidence:** WHO describes a case series as patient characteristics not compared with a reference population, often prompting fuller study ([WHO Basic, ch. 3, p. 39](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/47b3f1a2-d30c-418c-960c-5f8d80011432/content) [Direct]), and PAHO files it as descriptive ([MOPECE 5, pp. 5-7](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/628f8cce-746c-4890-b818-cd6d9af2f116/content) [Direct]); CDC's course does not use the term ([SS1978 L1 §7](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section7.html) [Keyword]). Case definitions change as knowledge grows, as SARS did during 2003 ([SS1978 L1 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section5.html) [Direct]), which bears on CS1. The Manual gives a definition three parts, a clinical or laboratory condition, a period and a setting, and expects it to be revised and narrowed ([King et al., Manual ch. 3, Step 4](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/field-investigation.html) [Direct]), so CS1 should date its definition. Beyond that, definitions conflict three ways. Dekkers samples on outcome (reported by JBI and Mathes and Pieper; Dekkers [Not read]). GRADE's glossary describes patients usually all receiving one intervention ([GRADE Handbook](https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html) [Tool]). Murad says the uncontrolled nature and small size define a series, not sampling ([Murad 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjebm-2017-110853) [Full]). JBI's ten items and Murad's eight are the published instruments, and JBI's options Yes / No / Unclear / Not applicable are source wording ([JBI 2017](https://www.unisa.edu.au/contentassets/72bf75606a2b4abcaf7f17404af374ad/6i-jbi_critical_appraisal-checklist_for_case_series20171.pdf) [Full]). Murad advises against an aggregate score. Series give proportions and event rates, not odds ratios or relative risks; Murad's "event rates" hold only with a defined denominator, which is my reconciliation. **Strength:** Moderate for CS1 to CS3 as guidance, Thin for CS4, with no agreement data on the JBI and Murad tools found. **Origin:** CS1 and CS2 adapted from JBI items 1 to 5; CS3 Constructed; CS4 adapted from Murad's causal items.

### Surveillance

**PAHO's definition says the quiet part plainly.** Surveillance methods are chosen for being practical, uniform and rapid rather than for accuracy or completeness; it is a continuing process of watching trends and comparing what is seen with what is expected, as information for action ([MOPECE 4, 3rd ed.](https://iris.paho.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/31ca7682-c8d6-4c1b-846c-41ea374f0363/content) [Direct, in Spanish], PDF p. 18, after Porta 2014). That is why a trend is the claim surveillance data can carry, and why a count is not a census. The same module separates passive, active, sentinel and syndromic surveillance, each with a stated trade-off (PDF p. 22), which S11 uses.

**The objective comes first, because it decides what counts as good enough.** [Groseclose and Buckeridge 2017](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-publhealth-031816-044348) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner] gives the standard definition of surveillance as ongoing, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation tied to dissemination and action (p. 58, quoting Thacker and Berkelman), which supports treating it as a system and not a study type (D1, decision 8). Its Table 1 sets out ten objectives, from guiding immediate action to providing a basis for research. Data quality need only suit the objective (p. 66), and a review it cites found the objective was often not stated (p. 68). Table 3 defines the attributes used here: sensitivity for cases, for outbreaks and of the case definition; predictive value positive; representativeness; timeliness; data quality. Raising sensitivity may lower predictive value positive (p. 68). The review calls aiding interpretation perhaps the most important use of monitoring data (p. 66). It is a narrative review, and its disclosure says it is not an official CDC position. S8 to S10 are constructed from it.

**Evidence:** CDC's Lesson 5 is the best agency list of artefacts: passive to active reporting, case definition change, publicity, more diagnosis, new tests, physician awareness, a similar disease misdiagnosed, laboratory error, batch reporting, population growth and duplicates, with resort and college towns adding sudden numerator shifts ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]; [SS1978 L6 §2, Step 2](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). Its figures show a true salmonellosis rise and a definition-driven AIDS rise looking alike. It says rates are preferred and a denominator is a critical prior step, that saying incidence rose requires comparison over time or across areas, and that the increase warranting action is set locally, which supports refusing numeric thresholds ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]). **It separates acting from claiming:** officials usually treat an apparent increase as real and respond until proven otherwise ([SS1978 L5 §5](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/section5.html) [Direct]). German also names heightened awareness, new diagnostic tests and changed surveillance methods as causes of changed sensitivity ([German 2001, Task D.2.e](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5013a1.htm) [Direct]), and the Manual requires spurious rises, called pseudoepidemics, to be ruled out before an outbreak is confirmed ([King et al., Manual ch. 3, Step 3](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/field-investigation.html) [Direct]). I found no CDC text in the sources read stating when surveillance data support an analytic or causal claim: German lists evaluation among the uses and gives no conditions, and ch. 8's causal discussion is about analytic studies.

Measured cases back this up. Seven COVID-19 definition versions changed the detected proportion by 7.1-fold, 2.8-fold and 4.2-fold, with about 232,000 cases estimated under version 5 against 55,508 reported, from a simple exponential model the authors say cannot separate interventions and testing capacity ([Tsang 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(20)30089-X) [Full]). England's move to episodes added about 4.0%, or 588,114 cases ([UKHSA blog](https://ukhsa.blog.gov.uk/2022/02/04/changing-the-covid-19-case-definition/) [Direct]), and UKHSA flags testing policy, a step change in influenza ascertainment and a system migration as trend caveats ([UKHSA DQ](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sources-of-surveillance-data-for-influenza-covid-19-and-other-respiratory-viruses/data-quality-report-national-flu-and-covid-19-surveillance-report) [Direct]). Its change log is a model for dated revisions ([UKHSA HCAI QMI](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/hcai-qmi-report/quality-and-methodology-information-qmi-for-healthcare-associated-infections-hcai-reports) [Direct]). Completeness cannot be borrowed: multipliers ran 0.4 to 2,082.9 for salmonellosis and 0.4 to 39,000 for campylobacteriosis, and capture-recapture corrects underreporting and not under-ascertainment ([Gibbons 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4015559/) [Tool, first 100,000 of 177,160 characters]). Delayed death reporting, with a 3-week delay and 2-week doubling time, understates case fatality about 2.8-fold, and preferential detection of severe cases inflates it ([Lipsitch 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4504518/) [Tool]). Lipsitch and Gibbons address case fatality and foodborne multipliers, so applying them to trend claims in S2 and S4 is by extension. Right truncation hides recent cases ([McGough 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007735) [Full]). A statistical signal prompts checks and does not define an outbreak ([Noufaily 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/bty997) [Full]; [SS1978 L6 §2, Step 2](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct]). Buehler adds that a statistical deviation from baseline can trigger investigation and is not enough to define an outbreak, which a jurisdiction defines by whether occurrence has changed enough to warrant attention ([Buehler 2004, B.2, Validity](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5305a1.htm) [Direct]); that framework concerns system evaluation, and its support for S5 is by application. **This corrects S3.** German says denominators often come from a separate system, and asks that numerators and denominators be comparable across categories and that the denominator's source stay consistent when measuring trends ([German 2001, Task D.2.g](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5013a1.htm) [Direct]). The earlier options treated any other source as approximate, which would have marked ordinary census denominators down; they now turn on comparability. German also calls the share of unknown or blank responses a simple measure of data quality and says a full assessment may need a special study (Task D.2.c), which agrees with S2's blanks option. For S7, the Manual offers the period just before or the same period a year earlier, and warns of varying definitions and incomplete reporting ([King et al., Manual ch. 3, Step 3](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/field-investigation.html) [Direct]). For S6, it prefers onset, notes surveillance systems may have only a report date, and sets epidemic-curve intervals at half an incubation or latency period or less, shrinking as cases rise ([Fontaine, Manual ch. 6](https://www.cdc.gov/field-epi-manual/php/chapters/describing-epi-data.html) [Direct]). The claim that bin choice changes visible peaks stays unconfirmed: the Manual gives the interval rule without saying so, Lesson 6's figures show one outbreak at different intervals, and only the captions were read ([SS1978 L6 §2, Step 6](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson6/section2.html) [Direct, captions]). **Strength:** Moderate for S1 and S4, with firmer support than before; Moderate for S2 on the caveat that completeness cannot be borrowed; Moderate for S3, its comparability rule now read in German; Moderate for S5, firmer; Moderate for S6 on the Manual's date and interval guidance, and Thin on what bin choice does to a curve; Moderate for S7 on two agency passages. **Origin:** all Constructed. I found no signalling tool for surveillance-based inference, and GRADE was silent in the portions read.

### Any design, where outcomes are not independent

The same assumption fails in a second way: outcomes within a house, school or area can be correlated even without spread ([Diez-Roux 1998](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.88.2.216) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], p. 220). `two_by_two` computes its intervals as if observations were independent, so clustered outcomes make them too narrow, which is this report's inference. It bears on deaths counted by house.


**Evidence:** [Susser 1994](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.84.5.825) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], pp. 826-828. With a transmissible infection, an individual's risk depends on how common the infection already is, so an outcome in one person is not independent of another's. Susser cites Koopman's HIV model, where the odds ratio for high-risk behaviour changes with prevalence, and calls the individual measure unstable. In dengue in Mexico, larvae counts showed no association with infection at individual level, while households and communities with mosquitoes carried raised risk. No tree or tool read asks this, and it applies to every design that reports a measure. **Strength:** Moderate as a caveat, resting on one paper's argument and its cited examples, which were not read. **Origin:** Constructed.

## South London shows the tree without a seventh design

**`Deaths per house, by water company` keeps `cohort` only as a stated reading.** Snow says house supply was fixed by the owner or occupier's decision when companies competed, so people did choose; the argument is that the choice predated the 1852 intake move and was unrelated to the later contamination contrast, and "assigned at random" would be wrong ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]; [Dunning 2008, p. 18](https://zenodo.org/records/996336/files/Dunning_2008_Natural_QMMR_6_2.pdf) [Full]). The research found no authority calling this a cohort, and the term "cohort study" was proposed in 1960 ([Morabia 2001](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.1007/bf01318790/full) [Full]). The comparison has cohort logic, but exposure was looked up only for deaths, and the denominator is a company count from another source. Three further limits matter. The 40,046 and 26,107 are whole-company counts spanning sub-districts served by one company, so the as-if argument covers less than the 315 against 37 headline; I saw no read source make this point. The row is a `supplier`, and `deaths` over `houses` is not a proportion of houses, as the workflow's own `rateKind:"ratio"` concedes, so the licence sentence "a/(a+b) is a risk" does not describe it. And the ratio moves with period and denominator: 8.40 on seven-week houses by my recomputation, about 14 for four weeks by Snow's rounded rates, about 6 for the whole epidemic per population, and about 3.5 on Simon's linked figures, which Snow attributes to address errors ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]; [Snow 1856](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66507) [Full/Tool]). Vandenbroucke notes that Snow's second edition plays down part of the data by emphasising the first part of the epidemic ([Vandenbroucke 2001](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.1007/bf01321079/full) [Full]). A note would state:

`Declared as a cohort study, 1854. The unit of analysis is the house. Exposure was linked to each death; the denominator was a count of houses for each supplier from a return to Parliament. Exposure was allocated outside the investigator's control by the companies' supply, argued to be unrelated to other causes of the outcome because the choice of supply predated the 1852 intake move. This is an argument and not a demonstration, and it applies to the intermixed sub-districts.`

**`Died or survived, by water supply` is the cleanest case.** Companies attach to sub-districts, so the design is `ecological`, and Snow treats the area table as a prompt for the more decisive intermixed test. Snow's question was about individuals, so E1 applies and the result is a lead, not an individual effect. Freedman calls the area comparisons ecological and says Snow was careful to avoid the fallacy ([Freedman 1999, TR 521](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ed260/freedman521.pdf) [Full]). The README's 5.35 on people and 300,149 excluded people in the both-companies stratum are carried into E4:

`Declared as an ecological study, 1854. The unit of analysis is the sub-district. The claim concerns individuals, and these data are group-level, so a relation between groups is reported as a lead and not as an individual effect. Sub-districts supplied by both companies were excluded; they were not controlled for.`

**`Cholera in 1849 compared with 1854` needs no new design.** The draft treated it as a missing design. Under three records it is `ecological`, with allocation by the Lambeth Company's move and a before-and-after time record. In Morgenstern's terms it is a **mixed** design, the strongest of the paper's ecologic types, though that paper measures absolute change in rates and this workflow gives a ratio of counts ([Morgenstern 1982](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.72.12.1336) [Direct, PDF supplied by the practitioner], p. 1339). The population-intervention exception does not apply: in 1855 the water link was the thing in question, not established, so the claim stays etiologic and the ecological fallacy stays live. In E8's terms a sub-district's company is a group measure of a physical exposure, which Diez-Roux says is generally a proxy for individual exposure. In a sub-district served by one company it comes close to Susser's integral variable, but Snow reports pump-wells inside such areas, so it does not reach everyone, and in the jointly supplied ones it varies within the group. Both readings are this report's. Summing the file gives `deaths_1849` to `deaths_1854` of 2,261 to 2,458 for `Southwark and Vauxhall Company only` (1854 deaths per 1849 death 1.09) and 3,905 to 2,547 for `Lambeth Company and Southwark and Vauxhall Company` (0.65). For `Lambeth Company only` the research note gives 162 to 37 (0.23), while the file leaves `deaths_1854` blank for Dulwich, so summing only complete rows gives 161; I did not run the workflow to see which it shows. These are counts, deliberately, because the repository holds one population. Cunningham reads a modified Table XII as a precursor to DiD ([Cunningham](https://mixtape.scunning.com/09-difference_in_differences) [Tool]), and that is a modern reinterpretation, not Snow's analysis, so it should not be presented as a DiD estimate. Snow also flags that Lambeth's supply extended to Streatham, Norwood and Sydenham after 1849 and that Streatham's 1849 count reflected an asylum outbreak ([Snow 1855](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72894) [Full]), which compromises exactly the group that carries the contrast. Whether the sub-district comparison meets Dunning's narrow definition is doubtful in my reading, since Dunning's as-if argument is about intermixed houses. It meets the MRC line's broad one. So the README's sentence that the move upstream "makes this a natural experiment" is true under one definition and should name it.

`Declared as an ecological study, 1849 and 1854. The unit of analysis is the sub-district. Exposure was allocated outside the investigator's control by the Lambeth Company's move of its intake in 1852. deaths_1849 and deaths_1854 were compared against sub-districts whose supply did not change. These are counts, and no rate or difference-in-differences estimate is given. This is a natural experiment in the broad sense, exposure being outside the investigator's control (Craig et al. 2025). It is not thereby a natural experiment in the narrow sense, which requires random or as-if random assignment (Dunning 2008).`

Fig. 3 runs from a total population to exposed and unexposed groups where the available information allows the comparison.
