# Evidence splits the five forms in two

No open study teaches the five-way scheme (direct quote, paraphrase, précis, summary, own words), and none tests whether writers who label their own text this way write better or plagiarise less. The question cannot be answered for the scheme as proposed. The parts of it that have been studied give answers of uneven strength. **The literature classifies source use along two separate axes, not one list.** One axis is the form of borrowing (exact copy, patchwriting, paraphrase, summary, quotation). The other is integration: whether the writer only reports what sources say or takes a position by weighing them. "Own words" in the five-way sense belongs to the second axis. **No study found treats précis as a category distinct from summary.** On the outcomes, teaching summary writing produces better summaries in school-age students (ES 0.82, 4 studies). Explicit synthesis instruction raises undergraduates' integration of sources, but with small advantages over active comparisons. Short plagiarism and paraphrase interventions reduce plagiarism flagged by software, in six controlled studies that are mostly at high risk of bias. For public health trainees there is no study at all of source-use distinctions; their evaluated writing training reports publication counts, not quality. Against experiment 183's criteria, the evidence does not meet "validated". It answers the second criterion (do the forms match what was taught?) against a single five-item list. On the first criterion (does teaching these distinctions improve writing?) it cannot say anything for this scheme.

The notes behind this report were compiled on 2026-11-07 by three searches of about 13–20 tool calls each. Coverage is therefore narrow. ## The literature codes borrowing and argument on different axes

The schemes researchers actually use fall into three families, and none of them is the five-way list. The **Citation Project** (Jamieson & Howard; 174 first-year papers from 16 US institutions, 1,911 citations) coded each citation as exact copying, patchwriting, paraphrase, summary or quotation ([Jamieson & Howard](https://www.academia.edu/1646133/Writing_from_Sources_Writing_from_Sentences); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2011/04/11/skimming-surface)) [snippet]. Keck (2006) graded paraphrases by the share of words they keep from the source: Near Copy, Minimal Revision, Moderate Revision, Substantial Revision ([bepress](https://works.bepress.com/casey_keck/2/)) [abstract/snippet]. Both are borrowing-form schemes: they say how close a passage stands to its source.

The third family scores what the writer does with sources. The only systematic review of synthesis-writing instruction defines effective teaching by three "transformational processes": **selecting, organising and connecting** information from sources ([van Ockenburg et al. 2019](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/606/517/478)) [full]. The Spanish argumentative-synthesis group (Mateos, Luna, Villalón and colleagues) uses an ordinal integration scale. At the low end a text has no conclusion, or one that does not integrate the sources; at the top the writer takes "a position in support of one or both perspectives" and argues "by weighing or synthesizing" arguments from both throughout ([Luna et al. 2023, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8749917/fullTextXML)) [part]. That scale turns the distinction between reporting sources and arguing one's own position into a score, with inter-judge kappa of 0.90. The L2 citation-function literature adds a third reading: novices use citations mainly for plain attribution, and seldom to support or justify their own claims ([McCulloch 2012](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/62526/) [abstract]; [Sun, Kuzborska & Soden 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X2200046X) [abstract]).

Set beside these, the five-way scheme mixes the axes. Direct quote, paraphrase, précis and summary are steps of rewording and compression on the borrowing axis. Own words (argument, analysis, reasoning, synthesis) is the top of the integration axis. A passage can sit on both axes at once: a writer's argument often contains a paraphrase. A single list makes the writer choose one. **No validated instrument was found that crosses the two axes**, so a two-coordinate design would have no ready-made coding scheme to borrow. The precedent is simply that researchers keep the two apart.

**Précis has no footing in this literature.** No intervention teaches it as distinct from summary, and no coding scheme measures it separately. Graham & Perin's meta-analysis even folds multi-source synthesis (Knight 2003) into its "summarization" treatment ([Graham & Perin 2007](https://bridgestolearning2009.pbworks.com/f/graham&perin07.pdf)) [full]. This is an absence in the searched evidence, not a finding that précis is useless. Teaching traditions that use the term were not searched. **Patchwriting, by contrast, is a named, coded category.** In the Citation Project about 16% of citations were patchwritten, about 32% were paraphrase and only about 6% were summary, and **52% of papers had at least one patchwritten passage** ([UA Teaching Hub summary](https://teachinghub.as.ua.edu/?p=8624)) [snippet; percentages from secondary summaries, not checked against the primary text]. Keck found that L2 university writers produced significantly more Near Copies than L1 writers ([bepress](https://works.bepress.com/casey_keck/2/)) [abstract]. Pecorari found patchwriting in L2 doctoral dissertations and treats it as plagiarism without intent to deceive ([Springer record](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-981-287-079-7_68-1)) [snippet]. Howard first described it as a learning strategy, not dishonesty; the later working definition is "restating a phrase, clause, or one or more sentences while staying close to the language or syntax of the source" ([Nelms handout](https://teachingandlearninginhighered.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/nelms-patchwriting.pdf)) [snippet]. All of these studies are descriptive, not interventions.

## Summary and synthesis instruction improve the task taught, less clearly writing at large

The most-quoted number overstates what is known. Graham & Perin's **ES 0.82 for summarisation** rests on **4 effect sizes in grades 5–12**, and its quality outcome was "the completeness and accuracy of the written summary produced by students" ([Graham & Perin 2007](https://bridgestolearning2009.pbworks.com/f/graham&perin07.pdf)) [full]. It shows that teaching summary writing produces better summaries. It does not show better essays or arguments. Writing summaries also raised reading comprehension (ES 0.52, 19 studies, grades 1–12), but that figure comes from a synopsis and was not checked against the report's tables ([Writing to Read synopsis](https://core-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/documents/asset/uploaded_file/776383/Synopsis_Writing_to_Read.pdf)) [snippet]. In the one controlled school test of summary instruction alone as a route to synthesis, the effect on synthesis quality was negative (Hammann & Stevens 2003, **ES −0.34**, reported in [van Ockenburg et al. 2019](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/606/517/478)) [full]. That is weak but direct evidence that summary skill is not enough for synthesis.

In higher education the controlled evidence is a handful of small studies with mixed results. The van Ockenburg review lists undergraduate effects from **ES 1.10** (Zhang 2013, 29 ESL undergraduates, explicit discourse-synthesis instruction) and 0.88 (Robledo-Ramón 2016) down to 0.22 for a 10-minute session and **−0.86** for a digital writing environment. The authors did not pool them ([van Ockenburg et al. 2019](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/606/517/478)) [full]. The difference between strong and weak results lies in whether the instruction covered selecting, organising *and* connecting, with modelling and graphic organisers. Brief "integrating sources" sessions gave small effects.

The best randomised evidence on the reporting-versus-own-argument distinction is Luna et al. (2023). Sixty-six Spanish undergraduates were assigned to product-focused or process-focused online instruction. The process version used explicit instruction, video modelling and a script of critical questions. Both groups improved in integration (η²p = .38). The process group improved more, but the between-condition effect was small (**η²p = .074**, p = .027). The authors note that texts reaching medium or maximum integration "were still limited" ([Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8749917/fullTextXML)) [part]. Robledo & Álvarez (2024) report large gains in holistic quality (η² = .34; .52 with follow-up) from eight sessions of strategic, self-regulated synthesis instruction. But they randomised only four intact classes, two per arm, and analysed students, so the causal estimate is probably overstated ([Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11249555/fullTextXML)) [part]. A study of low-performing undergraduate pairs credits gains in **argument integration** to the explicit-instruction component alone ([Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10318-x)) [abstract; authors not verified]. Nearly all of this comes from one research network working with Spanish L1 psychology and education students. Durable effects are seldom measured.

The evidence therefore supports a narrower claim than the question asks about. Explicit teaching of how to move from reporting sources to weighing them helps undergraduates do that a little more. **No study teaches writers to label passages of their own text as reported or as their own and then measures argument quality.** Fieldwork's form field would be exactly that design.

## Plagiarism interventions lower detection rates more than copying

The practitioner's view, recorded as said, is: "at least the forms will help guard against plagiarism." The evidence supports something close to it for *instruction*, and has nothing to say yet about *self-labelling*. The one systematic review, Miranda-Rodríguez et al. (2024), found **six controlled interventions (1,631 undergraduates)**. Most were quasi-experimental and at high risk of bias, and heterogeneity ruled out meta-analysis. The authors conclude only that interventions "may reduce plagiarism" ([Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1357853/full)) [full; primary studies not read, figures from the review's extraction]. The strongest design, Dee & Jacob 2012, randomised 28 courses. It tested a plagiarism-and-citation tutorial with **no paraphrase training**, and the control course had more plagiarism in 11 of 14 pairs (p = .027). Newton et al. 2014 is the study aimed squarely at **paraphrasing and patchwriting**. It was randomised and improved scores on a test task: avoiding patchwriting 4.30 vs 3.77, paraphrasing 3.49 vs 2.54. But it measured a pen-and-paper test, not real assignments, and only 137 of 260 enrolled students completed it ([same](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1357853/full)).

Two results bear directly on the form field. In Yang et al. 2019, assignments with any plagiarism fell from 79% to 48%, but **copied sentences barely moved (75% vs 68%, not significant)** ([same](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1357853/full)). Interventions appear to change citation behaviour more than how much source language writers reuse. And every outcome in the review was similarity software or expert review. **None measured patchwriting as Howard defines it**, which software does not separate cleanly from quotation or legitimate paraphrase. A single-group EAP study of 8 hours' source-use instruction found "modest overall improvement", with students "clearly not yet proficient" in "the more sophisticated and subtle aspects" ([record](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/0efc56d3-fa5b-4194-a38a-995dcb0931d3)) [abstract; paywalled, no open copy].

The following is inference, not finding. A writer who patchwrites usually believes they have paraphrased. Asked to choose a form, they will choose "paraphrase". Fieldwork usually does not hold the cited passage, so it cannot compare the two. On this evidence, a form label can make a writer **state** what they believe they did. It cannot **detect** patchwriting. Any guard against plagiarism would come from the writer's judgement at the moment of choosing, which is what Newton-style instruction trains. Whether a prompt at that moment does the same work has not been tested.

## Prompts help when they force judgement and fade with skill

The prompting literature favours prompts that make the writer do cognitive work over prompts that ask them to fill in a tag. Berthold, Nückles & Renkl (2007, N = 84) found that cognitive or combined prompts beat no prompts, and purely metacognitive prompts did not ([Uni Bielefeld record](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/1989095)) [snippet]. Students given permanent prompts over a term learned substantially less than students whose prompts were faded ([Nückles et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9106-9)) [snippet]. One long-term journal-prompting study found no effect ([Springer 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09671-x)) [snippet]. In 331 seventh graders, written claim–evidence–reasoning scaffolds that were faded produced stronger reasoning on unscaffolded items than permanent scaffolds ([record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/875707403897667770)) [snippet; paywalled, no open copy]. In grades 1–8, structured self-assessment improved writing quality (ES 0.62), more than computer feedback (0.38) ([ACU record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86vq0/formative-assessment-and-writing-a-meta-analysis)) [snippet]. The closest study to self-labelling citation functions is a single-case exploratory study, not an intervention ([Petrić & Harwood 2013, Essex](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/5465/)) [snippet].

The already-settled decision against a language model writing or rewriting notes fits this evidence. In a randomised writing study, ChatGPT support raised essay scores but **not knowledge gain or transfer** ([Fan et al., arXiv 2412.09315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315v1)) [abstract]. In 2023-era models, **55% (GPT-3.5) and 18% (GPT-4) of generated citations were fabricated** ([Walters & Wilder 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5.pdf)) [snippet/abstract]. Current-model rates are not established by these sources.

## Public health writing training measures output, not source use

For health and science trainees the evidence base is thin and does not touch source-use distinctions. A systematic review found only 17 comparative studies of training in writing for publication and peer review, "generally small and inconclusive" ([Galipeau et al. 2015, Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2014.09.024&resultType=core&format=json)) [abstract; paywalled, per-study outcomes not seen]. One small RCT (48 health professionals) rated manuscript quality, comparing online with face-to-face delivery of the same course ([Phadtare et al. 2009](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/1472-6920-9-27)) [abstract]. FETP evaluations report satisfaction and counts. About 20% of WPSAR workshop manuscripts were published in WPSAR ([PMC8521131](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8521131/)) [snippet]. Programmes name post-workshop mentorship and supervisor time as the binding constraint. **No FETP, MPH or medical-trainee study measuring how writers separate reported evidence from their own interpretation was found.**

What the health literature does show is where the writer's own argument goes wrong. Across 1,170 observational-study articles, **18.7% used strongly causal linking language**, and **in 44.5% the action recommendations implied more causality than the linking sentence** ([Haber et al., medRxiv](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.08.25.21262631v5.full.pdf)) [snippet]. Spin reaches up to 84% of non-randomised-trial reports ([Chiu, Grundy & Bero 2017](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:plo:pbio00:2002173)) [snippet]. A randomised editorial instruction against spin had no significant effect (57% vs 63%) ([Ghannad et al. 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.10.014&resultType=core&format=json)) [abstract]. **No trial of training authors to calibrate causal claims was found.** This overclaiming sits in the integration axis, not the borrowing axis. A trainee can quote and paraphrase faultlessly and still let a recommendation outrun an association. Fieldwork's function field (finding, limitation, and so on) already describes that layer. The form field describes the other one.

## Design options and their evidence

These are options for experiment 183, set out with the evidence for and against each. None is settled here.

| Option | Evidence for | Evidence against or missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep one five-item list** (direct quote, paraphrase, précis, summary, own words) | Simple to present; each item names a move that writing guides teach | No study uses it; it mixes the borrowing and integration axes and forces one choice where a passage can hold both; no outcome evidence |
| **Two coordinates**: degree of borrowing (quoted → patchwriting → paraphrase → summary) and reporting vs own argument | Matches how the research codes source use (Citation Project/Keck vs Mateos integration scale); Fieldwork's kind (quoted/authored) and function already cover parts of each | No validated instrument crosses the two; more for the writer to answer; no outcome evidence for labelling either one |
| **Keep précis** | It is a recognised exercise in some teaching traditions (not searched here) | No study distinguishes it from summary or measures it; Graham & Perin fold even synthesis into summarisation |
| **Drop or merge précis into summary** | Matches every taxonomy found | Removes a distinction some instructors may teach; the absence of evidence comes from a narrow search |
| **Name patchwriting** as a form or a flagged state | A coded category in the main descriptive dataset (about 16% of citations, 52% of papers); Newton et al. trained its avoidance and improved test performance | Writers seldom recognise their own patchwriting, so a self-chosen label may rarely be picked; Fieldwork cannot check it without the source text; Howard's framing as a learning stage argues for a neutral, non-accusatory name |
| **Leave form unstated by default, prompt and fade** | Faded scaffolds beat permanent ones (McNeill; Nückles); permanent prompts can hurt capable writers | Evidence is school-level or journal-writing, [snippet]; fading in Fieldwork's context is untested |
| **Prompt a judgement rather than a tag** (for example, "is this closer to the source's wording or your own?") | Cognitive prompts beat purely metacognitive ones (Berthold et al.); self-assessment ES 0.62 | No study of this prompt in source-based writing; [snippet] evidence |

## Conclusion

The question bundles two claims. The evidence treats them very differently. That writers benefit from being taught to tell borrowing apart from argument has modest support in undergraduates. That one list of five forms is the way to teach it has none. The research separates the axes, and the axis with the clearest public health stakes is the integration one, where findings turn into recommendations that outrun them. The form of borrowing is the axis plagiarism interventions target, and even there the measured gain is in citation behaviour more than in copied language. Whether experiment 183 is retired or kept open turns on which criterion governs. The forms do not match the field's categories as a single list. Whether teaching them improves writing is a question nobody has tested.

Fieldwork's position is unusual in a way that matters for any test it runs. It has no access to the source text, so a form label reports the writer's belief about their passage rather than a checked fact. This makes a label's value depend on whether choosing it makes the writer compare their sentence with the source, which is a testable claim. The open questions most worth answering are these. Does a form prompt change how close writers stay to a source's wording when someone holding the passage checks the result? Does it change later unprompted writing? Does a patchwriting option get chosen at all? None of these is established, and no amount of further literature search in these areas is likely to settle them for this setting.
