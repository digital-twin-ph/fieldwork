# Notes

_Created 2026-11-07 · Updated 2026-11-18_

## What it does

The Notes tab is the project's write-up. It shows every note from every workflow in the project, filed under the four parts of the body (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion), followed by References.

A note is one of four kinds:

- **quoted**: an author's own sentence, carried verbatim.
- **derived**: restated from what an input declares, such as its source.
- **machined**: written by a widget about what it did; the next run rewrites it.
- **authored**: written by you.

Each note has a **function**, which says what it does for a reader (context, rationale, objective, source, method, verification, finding, limitation). An authored note can also answer two questions about its sources: **whose words** and **whose argument**.

## How to use it

1. Choose **Add note** under the part of the write-up where the note belongs.
2. Write the note. The dialog shows questions to ask of that part; hide them once they are habit.
3. Choose its function. If it draws on literature, answer whose words and whose argument.
4. Add the works it cites under **Citations**. They are marked on the note and listed in References.
5. To show a picture with the note, open **Image** and choose a PNG, JPEG, WebP or SVG file. Write its **alt text**,
   what the picture shows for someone who cannot see it, and its **caption**, what a reader is to take from it. Both
   are required.
6. Choose **Add note** (or **Save** when editing) to keep it on the project.

A picture goes above the note's words, on the board and in the write-up. It is kept on this device by its digest, and
an exported project carries it. A note whose picture is not on this device says so, with its caption.

A note can be moved to another part by dragging its card. Editing a machined note does not change it: the edit becomes an authored note that keeps the wording it was taken from.

## The parts of the write-up

Each part has a job, and the questions under it are for checking a draft, not for structuring one. They are the same questions the Add note dialog shows for that part.

### Introduction

Shows the gap in knowledge, why filling it matters, and what this study adds. Build it in two moves: the problem (background to the gap, why it matters, what earlier studies left open), then the purpose (the approach, what is new in it). Funnel from broad to narrow and end on the objective. About three paragraphs. It is not a history of the issue or a full literature review, and it holds no results.

- What is the gap in knowledge, and why does filling it matter?
- What did earlier studies leave open that this study addresses?
- Does the last sentence state the objective?
- Is anything here background a reader does not need, or a result?

### Methods

Says exactly how the study was done, so someone could repeat it and judge it. Use subheadings, define every variable and how it was measured, describe original methods in detail and cite standard ones. A flow diagram can show how the rows or people studied were reached. Describe what was done, not what was found: a result belongs here only where it explains a choice of method, with the reason first.

- Could someone repeat the study from what is written here?
- Is every variable defined, with how it was measured?
- Does every method have a result that answers it?
- Is anything here a finding rather than what was done?

### Results

States the findings that bear on the objective, without interpreting them. Mirror the Methods: every measurement described there has a finding here, in the same order. Let the tables and figures carry the numbers, and say in a sentence or two what each shows. Use the past tense.

- Does each result answer a method, in the same order?
- Is any sentence interpreting rather than reporting?
- Does the text say in a sentence or two what each table or figure shows, without restating it?
- Do the numbers add up, and agree with the tables?

### Discussion

Interprets the results and justifies the interpretation, against what other studies found and the gap the study set out to fill. One plan is four paragraphs: the key result and the main conclusion; the results beside comparable studies; the major limitations, stated plainly; and recommendations for practice and for further study. It holds no new results.

- Is each conclusion beside the result that supports it?
- Does any conclusion or recommendation go further than the results support?
- How do these results compare with similar studies?
- Are the major limitations stated plainly, with what was done about them?

### References

Assembled from what the notes cite: a source an input declares, a method a computation uses, and a work a note cites from its Citations tab. Cite a work from the note that relies on it.

These parts and their questions follow the CDC/TEPHINET scientific writing course for Field Epidemiology Training Program residents, in this application's own words.

## Whose words, and whose argument

A note that draws on published work answers two questions. They are separate: a note can use a source's exact words and still be your argument, or be in your own words and only report.

**Whose words?** How much of the source's wording the note carries.

| Answer | What it means | A reader checks it against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **the source's exact words** | A quotation. Put it in quotation marks and cite the page | The source, word for word |
| **close to the source's wording** | Your sentence still follows the source's words and order. Common while learning to paraphrase; revise it into your own words, or quote it | The passage, which it may copy more than it should |
| **my words: one passage** | A paraphrase of one passage | That passage |
| **my words: the main points** | A summary of one work or several | Each work it cites |
| **not sure** | You looked and could not tell | A reviewer can help decide |

**Whose argument?** Whether the note reports the works or argues from them.

| Answer | What it means | A reader judges it by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **what the works say** | Reporting what others found | Whether the works say it |
| **what I make of them** | Your analysis, reasoning or synthesis, which may cite works in support | Whether the reasoning holds, and whether the works support it |
| **not sure** | You looked and could not tell | A reviewer can help decide |

Both are optional and read **unstated** until answered. A note on your own data, a step or a result draws on no literature and needs neither. When a note both reports and argues, consider writing two notes, one reporting and one with your reading, and linking them, so each can be checked on its own terms.

### What each answer commits you to

- **Exact words**: the passage itself, copied exactly. If the exact words do not matter, put it in your own.
- **One passage**: read the passage in full. Watch for staying so close to its wording that it becomes "close to the source's wording".
- **The main points**: read enough of each work to state its main point; an abstract can serve if the note says so. Watch for making works agree more than they do.
- **What I make of them**: make the turn from the works to your reading visible (*which suggests*, *for this study, that means*). Watch for claiming more than the evidence allows, most of all causation from an association.

### Examples

- **The source's exact words**, from Snow, 1855: "no experiment could have been devised which would more thoroughly test the effect of water supply on the progress of cholera than this".
- **Close to the source's wording**, the same passage: *No experiment could have been devised which would test the effect of the water supply on cholera more thoroughly than this one.* The wording and order are still Snow's.
- **My words: one passage**, the same passage: *Because the two companies' customers did not differ in their houses or their surroundings, the arrangement circumstances had produced tested the water supply more thoroughly than any designed experiment could have.*
- **My words: one passage**, from Murdock and colleagues, 2017, on their summer measurements: *In summer, the city's weather station read warmer than loggers in the vegetation where mosquitoes rest, by 3.0 to 4.2 °C in daily maximum.*
- **My words: the main points**, of Mordecai and colleagues, 2017: *Transmission by* Aedes aegypti *peaks near 29 °C and stops below about 18 °C and above about 35 °C, with adult survival setting the upper limit.*
- **My words: the main points**, of Murdock 2017 and Wimberly 2020: *Where temperature was logged at the places mosquitoes rest, it differed from the weather station's by enough to change modelled transmission, and the difference varied with season and land cover.*
- **What I make of them**, from the Brazil example: *The leads match lag 0, so an association of that size arises without temperature causing cases, and lag 0 cannot be read as transmission.* It reports nothing a work says; it is a reading of this study's own results.

## The outline: what the project is writing towards

A project names what it is writing towards, with its question: in **New project**, or later on the **Project** tab
under **Writing towards**. One or more: a publication, a situation report, a surveillance report. None yet is fine.

Once one is named, Notes opens on its **outline**: every part those products need, in reading order. Each part says:

- **how much fills it**: the notes that fill it, or that it is written in the draft, or **empty**;
- **which products use it**, and the heading and length it takes in each: a background of 150 to 300 words in the
  publication is one or two sentences in the situation report.

Above the parts, each product's **coverage**: how many of its parts are filled.

A note fills a part by its section and its function together: a note in Methods whose function is *verification*
fills **Data checks**. **Write it**, beside an empty part, adds a note with that section and function already set.

**Objectives** are one note, function *objective*, listing its objectives as numbered items, each answerable by a result.
In the note's dialog, **Add an objective** adds an item; each can be moved up or removed.

A note's dialog also shows, under **In each target**, the heading and length its section and function take in each
product the project writes towards.

**Objectives come first; workflows answer them later.** A new project can write its objectives before it has any
workflow. Under the Objectives part, each objective says whether a note answers it, how many tables, charts and maps
support it, and which workflow answers it, or **no workflow yet**. **Write its answer** adds a note in Results that
answers that objective.

- **The objective a workflow answers** is chosen on the **Project** tab, under the workflow's name, once the project has
  objectives. A workflow answers one objective at most.
- **Answers**, in a Results note's dialog, says which objective the note answers.
- **Tables, charts and maps shown with this note**, in any note of your own: ticked ones are drawn after that note in
  the draft, wherever it sits, and not in their own place. A map can support a point in the Introduction.

In the draft's Results, each objective is a numbered subsection, in the objectives' order: the notes that answer it, then
the results of the workflow that answers it. Results that answer no objective come first. An objective with nothing yet
says what belongs there.

## The plan: what each part will hold

Once a project names what it is writing towards, Notes shows its **plan** above the outline. It has one part for each
slot of the outline, shared by the targets that use it. Each part starts empty, with the slot's heading and length.

- **Plan it** opens a box for what that part will hold. Write it before the analysis, in a line or two.
- **The objectives part is your objectives note.** It is the one part a product shows, as the numbered objectives.
- Each part says how much fills it so far: notes, objectives, or the draft's own part.
- **Mark the plan complete** when you judge it done. Fieldwork keeps a dated version of every part. If a part changes
  afterwards, the plan says so. **Undo complete** removes the mark and keeps the versions.
- Add or drop a target at any time. An added target brings its parts, empty. A dropped target's written parts stay,
  under *Planned for a target no longer chosen*.
- A part can list the **tables, charts and maps** it will show, one a line, such as *map: cases by village*. Each is
  marked made once a note that fills the part names a map, chart or table of that kind.
- **Start a note from this** copies a part's words into a new note of the part's section, ready to rewrite. The note
  keeps which part it came from.
- The plan says how many parts have notes and no plan: written, not planned.

The plan is never part of any product: not the draft, the publication, a report or a download.

## The notes audit

**Notes audit**, beside the draft buttons and in the draft's own bar, shows where a target's write-up has gaps. It
opens on request and never stops a draft. One target at a time, as tabs:

- How many of the target's parts are written, as a number and a bar.
- Each part marked *written* (●), *planned, not written* (◐) or *empty* (○), with the length it asks for. Where the
  length is given in words or sentences, the written part is counted against it: *shorter than asked*, *within the
  length* or *longer than asked*.
- Parts planned and not written; parts written and not planned; notes this target does not use.
- Each objective, answered or not, and how many tables, charts and maps support it.
- How many tables, charts and maps there are, and how many a note names as resting on them.
- How many works are cited, and how many are named but not read.

It scores no sentence and writes none.

## Draft as a document

**Publication**, at the top of Notes, lays the write-up out as a document on a page, to read and to print. **Draft as**
chooses the kind:

| Format | Follows | Its parts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Publication | a journal article | Title, abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, data availability, references, appendix |
| Situation report, WHO style | WHO situation reports | Report number, data as of, highlights, situation in numbers, background, epidemiological update, risk assessment, preparedness and response, recommendations, data notes, references |
| Situation report, OCHA style | UN OCHA situation reports | Report number, as of, highlights, key figures, situation overview, response, gaps and challenges, notes on the data, references, contacts |
| Surveillance report, ECDC style | ECDC annual epidemiological reports | Reporting period, key facts, introduction, methods, epidemiology, discussion, public health implications, references |
| Surveillance summary | CDC's surveillance summaries | Period covered, summary, introduction, methods, results, discussion, limitations, public health action, references |
| Full report, with a three-question summary | CDC's full reports | A summary box of three questions (what is already known, what this adds, implications for practice), abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, limitations, public health implications, references |

Each format is filled from the same notes. The parts you write, such as highlights or recommendations, are written in
place with **Write**; until then the part shows what it is for and how long it runs, and prints nothing. **What you write
follows you between formats**: highlights written for a WHO-style sit rep are the key facts of an ECDC-style report. A
part one format has and another does not is named on screen, not lost.

**Each figure and table has a width and a place**, set above it:

- **Width**: full, half or a third of the text column.
- **Place**: on its own line; side by side with the next figure, which is drawn beside it; or with the text running
  round it, on the left or the right, as in Word; or left out of this kind of document.

The format and each figure's place are kept with the project, a figure's place separately for each format, so it can
be half width in the situation report and full width in the publication. The controls do not print. **Print or save as PDF** prints
the document alone. A worked example that ships with Fieldwork shows its document as it was set, with no controls.

**What a draft attaches rather than prints:**

- **A table too long to print whole**, more than 50 rows such as a line list, is attached as a CSV file. A line in its
  place names it: *Table S1. Each case with its chain, 162 rows: attached as S1-each-case-with-its-chain.csv.*
- **In the two situation reports, the surveillance summary and the full report**, equations and the tables an input
  reads go to an annex file, `annex.html`, and one line at the end of the draft says what it holds. The publication and
  the ECDC-style report keep their equations printed.
- **Rows fetched from elsewhere are never attached.** Where a workflow's table was fetched, as NYC's is from Zenodo,
  the line says where it came from and no file is written.

**Download the attached files** gives the annex and the CSV files in one zip. The PDF comes from **Print or save as
PDF**; a browser cannot put it in the zip.

**In print**, a heading over a part you have not written does not print, a table wraps rather than being cut at the
margin, and the date under the title says *Drafted*: it is the day the draft was made, not the date of the data.
Findings about the data, such as columns with no stated meaning, are stated once under the methods or data notes, not
as the study's limitations.

## What it will not do

- It does not check that a note is faithful to the works it cites, or that "my words" are. That is for the author and the reader; it seldom holds the source's text.
- It does not quote for you. A sentence carried word for word from a source belongs in quotation marks, with its page.
- The questions do not grade a note. A note that answers all of them can still be wrong.
- It does not check that alt text describes the picture, or that you may publish it. The picture's source and terms
  belong in the note or its citations.
